Alternative Data and Complex Systems

Alternative data and complex systems expand what can be observed while magnifying representativeness, licensing and identification problems; the method's value lies in stating the limits.

ModuleRES-M13 · 另类数据与复杂系统
LevelFoundation
Read6 min · 13 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Problem

Alternative data and complex systems address whether data from satellites, text, transaction records, sensors or networks can represent the target population, whether it may legally be used, and whether networks, feedback and simulations can support causal or predictive conclusions. The method's value lies in stating limits, not in treating data volume as truth[1][3][5][6].

Inputs

Inputs include data source and collection mechanism, covered population and period, missingness, licensing and privacy constraints, spatial and network structure, and simulation parameters. Alternative data are often non-probability samples: not randomly drawn but generated by platforms, devices or behavior[3].

Assumptions

Assumptions include representativeness for the target population, non-systematic missingness, collection that does not change observed behavior, licences permitting the current purpose, and identifiable network or simulation parameters. Selection bias arises when sample entry correlates with the target variable; simple weighting may not fix it.

Privacy and licensing are not compliance appendices but part of data usability: without lawful authority, analysis cannot be used; without privacy protection, data can harm the observed[2][4].

Steps

Steps fall into four groups: define the question (target population and analytical question); check coverage (collection mechanism, licensing and privacy); handle missingness and structure (missing mechanism, selection bias, network structure); and validate externally (other times, places and sources, with uncertainty reported)[3][5][6].

Example: bike-share data from one city cover 60% of the operating area but omit suburbs and older users. Inferring citywide travel demand requires stating the coverage bias and cross-checking against travel surveys or population data.

Figure

A coverage example: a city’s bike-share data record 120,000 trips inside the covered area, which is 60% of the operating area. Scaling directly as 120,000/0.6 gives 200,000 citywide; but uncovered suburbs and older users may ride less, so the true total might be 180,000. The 20,000 gap is not a rounding error: it is the untested assumption that the uncovered 40% behaves like the covered 60%. Better practice is to cross-check against travel surveys, transit card data or mobile signalling and report an interval.

Interpreting results

Interpreting results must separate description, correlation and causation: high network centrality is not large causal influence; simulation results depend on parameters and boundaries; conflicting multi-source measures should be reported, not averaged away. Feedback and non-linearity in complex systems invalidate linear extrapolation; interpretation needs conditions and time scale[5][6].

Failure conditions

Failure conditions include a sample unlike the target population; non-random missingness; collection changing behavior; licences not covering the use; wrong edge definitions; unidentifiable simulation parameters or insufficient calibration; conflicting measurement definitions. Failure often occurs before the data enter the model[4][5][6].

Reproduction

Reproduction requires saving licences, collection dates, cleaning rules, network-construction code and simulation parameters; external validation with independent sources; reporting privacy handling and access control. A “reproduction” without licence and privacy records is incomplete[1][2][4][6].

Knowledge-point review

representativeness

Definition: Representativeness means a sample reflects the population on key characteristics so findings can generalise.

Mechanism: It depends on sampling frame, coverage, response and trait distribution; weighting and stratification help but cannot fix a missing population.

Example: For example, a national stratified income survey must cover urban and rural, sectors and ages.

Boundary: Representativeness is not sample size; a large biased sample is unrepresentative, online and volunteer samples skew, and trait choice affects comparability.

selection bias

Definition: Selection bias occurs when inclusion in the sample differs from the population, through survivorship, self-selection, non-response and poor coverage.

Mechanism: It comes from sample definition, participation incentives and data availability; design, weighting, Heckman methods or bounds can mitigate.

Example: For example, surveying only responders or using only surviving funds biases the sample optimistically.

Boundary: Selection bias is not random error; it does not vanish with larger samples, weighting needs a correct model, unobserved selection is hard to fix, and extrapolation needs caution.

privacy and consent

Definition: Privacy and consent respect individual rights, informed consent and legal permissions when collecting, using and sharing personal data.

Mechanism: Privacy uses minimisation, anonymisation, aggregation, access control and compliance review; consent covers authorisation, secondary use and cross-border transfer.

Example: For example, research uses de-identified data with consent under personal-information law.

Boundary: Privacy is not deleting names; quasi-identifiers re-identify, aggregation can leak, consent scope and secondary use are often missed, and compliance is not ethical sufficiency.

spatial and network data

Definition: Spatial data carry location and adjacency, network data nodes and edges, and both have dependence that violates independent sampling.

Mechanism: Spatial autocorrelation, network homophily and clustering reduce effective sample size; spatial models, clustered errors or network sampling are needed.

Example: For example, nearby house prices correlate, behaviour spreads in social networks, and individuals are not independent.

Boundary: Spatial and network data are not ordinary tables; boundaries, distance and tie definitions matter, privacy and coverage are complex, and ignoring dependence understates errors and biases causality.

simulation and multi-source measurement

Definition: Simulation generates data from a model to test methods, and multi-source measurement measures one concept from different sources for cross-validation.

Mechanism: Simulation controls mechanisms and extremes but needs assumptions; multi-source data assess consistency, bias and measurement error.

Example: For example, simulated fat-tailed data test a strategy, and survey and administrative data cross-check income.

Boundary: Simulation is not reality; assumptions drive results and synthetic data can copy bias; multiple sources are not truth, and correlated sources and definition gaps create false agreement.

Exercises

  1. Concept check: explain the difference between a non-probability and a random sample and its effect on inference.
  2. Reasoning: if bike-share data cover 60% of the area in the example, how might the missing 40% affect demand estimates?
  3. Conditions: give an example where high network centrality has no causal influence.
Reference answers
  1. A random sample has known inclusion probabilities and design-based standard errors; a non-probability sample has unknown inclusion probabilities, is shaped by platforms, devices or behaviour, and cannot use random-sampling formulas directly. Simple weighting may not fix selection bias.
  2. If the uncovered 40% travels less, 120,000/0.6 overstates demand; if it travels more, it understates. State the coverage bias first, then cross-check with travel surveys, transit card data or mobile signalling and report an interval, not a point.
  3. An airport may have high degree in the aviation network, but that reflects geography and route allocation, not a causal effect on local GDP; network position is an outcome or mediator, not automatically a cause.
