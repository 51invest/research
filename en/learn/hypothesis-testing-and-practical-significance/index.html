Hypothesis Testing and Practical Significance

Hypothesis testing treats “no difference” as the null and asks whether the sample supports rejecting it; a small p-value means the data are inconsistent with the null, not that the effect is large, important or real.

Research question

Using a hypothetical 36-month sample: mean 1.20%, standard deviation 2.40%. Did the strategy produce a mean return above zero? If the minimum economic requirement, fixed in advance, is 0.60% monthly, can the same data establish practical significance? This article separates statistical from practical significance and explains p-values, significance levels and the two types of error. All numbers are hypothetical.

Data

Item Value
Sample size n 36
Sample mean 1.20% per month
Sample standard deviation 2.40 percentage points
Standard error 0.40 percentage points
Null hypothesis H0 μ = 0
Alternative Ha μ ≠ 0
Practical threshold (pre-set) 0.60% per month

Observations are hypothetical; the threshold was fixed before seeing the data.

Method and assumptions

The test statistic is:

t = (x̄ - μ0) / (s / √n)

Under H0 it follows a t distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom. For a two-sided test at α = 0.05, reject H0 if |t| > t(0.975, 35) = 2.030 or p < 0.05 (OpenStax 9.1, 9.5). α is the probability of rejecting a true null; β is the probability of failing to reject a false null and rises as the difference shrinks (OpenStax 9.2; NIST 7.1.3 notes small differences are harder to detect and β rises as α falls). Assumptions: simple random sampling, independent observations, approximate normality, and a pre-specified μ0 and threshold. Power is the probability of correctly rejecting a false H0, which OpenStax writes as 1−β (OpenStax 9.2). The significance level governs the false-alarm rate and power governs the chance that a real difference is detected at a given sample size, so the 0.60% practical threshold must be fixed in advance: the closer the threshold is to zero, the harder it is to raise power at n=36, and only a larger sample or a better design helps.

Results

SE = 0.40.

t = (1.20 - 0)/0.40 = 3.00, df = 35.

Two-sided p ≈ 0.005; the 95% interval is 0.39% to 2.01%, excluding 0. At α = 0.05 we reject H0: the data are inconsistent with zero.

The threshold is 0.60%: a one-sided test gives t = 1.50, p ≈ 0.071, so we cannot reject μ ≤ 0.60% at 5%. The result is statistically distinguishable from zero but does not establish the 0.60% economic threshold. p = 0.005 is neither the probability that H0 is true nor that the effect is real.

Robustness

At α = 0.01, t = 3.00 exceeds 2.724, so the conclusion that the mean is not zero is unchanged; practical significance still fails. A one-sided μ > 0 test gives p ≈ 0.0025, the same conclusion. With n = 100, same mean and s, SE = 0.24, t = 5.00 and the 95% interval is 0.72% to 1.68%; 0.60% now lies outside it, changing the practical conclusion. Testing many strategies, periods or thresholds accumulates false positives; pre-register hypotheses and report all attempts. Serial correlation makes p-values too small; bias shifts the estimate.

Limitations

p-values depend on the design and model; they do not measure effect size, economic value or causation. Significance can come from a large sample and a tiny difference, while practical significance depends on costs, risk, capacity and opportunity cost. Choosing thresholds after seeing results, reporting only significant findings and treating correlation as causation overstate evidence. Numbers are hypothetical.

Reproduction

  1. State H0, Ha, α and the practical threshold first.
  2. Compute n, x̄, s, SE, t and degrees of freedom.
  3. Obtain a two-sided or one-sided p-value.
  4. Compare with α and state the conclusion's strength.
  5. Report the interval, effect size and all tests.
se = s / sqrt(n)
t = (mean - mu0) / se
p_two = 2 * (1 - pt(abs(t), df=n-1))

Strength of conclusions

In this hypothetical sample, mean return differs from zero at the 5% level (t = 3.00, p ≈ 0.005, 95% interval 0.39% to 2.01%), but the data do not establish the 0.60% threshold (one-sided p ≈ 0.071), nor that the strategy works or has a causal effect.

Exercises

  1. Does p = 0.04 mean the effect is important?
  2. As the sample grows, how does p change? Does practical significance follow?
  3. Explain α and β, and why a fixed sample cannot reduce both at once.
Suggested answers
  1. No. The p-value is the chance of the observed or a more extreme result under H0; importance depends on effect size, interval and costs.
  2. A larger sample lowers SE, raises t and lowers p; practical significance depends on the threshold, not p alone.
  3. α is the chance of rejecting a true null; β the chance of not rejecting a false null. Lowering α raises β at fixed n; reducing both needs more data.
