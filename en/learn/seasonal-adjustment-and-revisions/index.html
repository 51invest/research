Seasonal Adjustment and Revisions

Seasonal adjustment strips out calendar-driven swings, and the adjusted numbers are themselves revised; a monthly change smaller than the typical revision is not yet evidence of a trend.

Research question

December retail sales are 25% above October, so is demand stronger, or is this seasonality? The first release shows a seasonally adjusted change of 0.0%, and the next release cuts the December adjusted level by 0.6%. The question is when one release's adjusted monthly change can support a trend claim, and when the answer must wait for revisions. Scope: the rules that govern adjustment and revisions; all numbers are hypothetical.

Data

Suppose a monthly retail index with seasonal and calendar effects, adjusted multiplicatively: adjusted = raw ÷ seasonal factor.

After revision: December raw is 132.3 with a factor of 1.21, so the adjusted value is 132.3 ÷ 1.21 = 109.3; November raw is 119.0 with a factor of 1.08, giving 110.2.

Method and assumptions

Adjustment assumes a series combines trend-cycle, seasonal (including calendar) and irregular behaviour: additive Y = C + S + I, multiplicative Y = C × S × I. In the multiplicative case seasonal effects move proportionally with the level, in the additive case they stay broadly constant, and a multiplicative decomposition cannot handle zeros or negatives. Trading-day counts, moving holidays and leap years are calendar effects; outliers, level shifts and seasonal breaks should be identified before estimation. Revisions are planned improvements that add data, benchmark, update factors or rebase; corrections respond to identified errors. The examples assume consistent monthly definitions, factors estimated from a long enough history, and an irregular term equal to 1, so the three identical adjusted values simply demonstrate the division rather than describe a real series.

Seasonal adjustment removes systematic calendar-related variation so consecutive periods can be compared - the ONS page states verbatim "The purpose of seasonal adjustment is to remove systematic calendar-related variation associated with the time of the year, that is, seasonal effects. This facilitates comparisons between consecutive time periods.". A series decomposes into trend-cycle, seasonality including calendar effects, and the irregular component; the page writes "These three components are: systematic calendar-related effects, irregular fluctuations and trend behaviour." and gives the models as "additive model: Y equals C plus S plus I multiplicative model: Y equals C multiplied by S multiplied by I". Outliers, level shifts and seasonal spikes affect adjustment quality - the page likewise states "There are a variety of issues that can impact on the quality of the seasonal adjustment.", listing outliers, trend breaks (also known as level shifts) and seasonal breaks [1].

Results

The raw series rises 25.0% from October to December (105.6 to 132.0), while the adjusted series is 110.0 in all three months, a change of 0.0%: almost the entire rise is seasonal. With an additive model and a December seasonal component of +12.0, the adjusted value would be 132.0 - 12.0 = 120.0 rather than 110.0, so the model choice moves the level.

After revision, December is 109.3 and November 110.2, so the monthly change is 109.3 ÷ 110.2 - 1 ≈ -0.8%. The shift from 0.0% to -0.8% combines a 0.2% upward revision to the raw value with a 0.8% upward revision to the factor; the December adjusted level itself moves by 0.6%. Because the 0.8-percentage-point revision is as large as the monthly change, a single release cannot support a trend claim; look at cumulative changes over three months or more and compare them with the series' typical revision size.

Robustness

Compare the raw year-on-year change; use a three-month moving average to reduce endpoint noise; compare vintages and record each month's value in every release; switch model or calendar adjustment. If a claim holds only in one vintage or under one model, report that.

A revision is a scheduled change to published output: routine recalculations include updated seasonal factors and benchmarking, annual reassessments typically incorporate major annual sources about 18 months after the year being measured, and annual chain-weighting moves the reference year, so volume series are revised [3].

Limitations

Seasonal factors are estimates and are least stable at the ends of a series; a changing seasonal pattern, a structural break or an unusual year can invalidate historical factors. Seasonal factors are least stable at the ends of a series, a general limitation of seasonal adjustment; the ONS page only discusses outliers, trend breaks and seasonal breaks, and does not expand on the endpoint problem [1]. Revisions carry their own error, and benchmarking can shift an entire history. Adjustment does not create information, it only rearranges variation, and it cannot explain why demand changed. The numbers are hypothetical and are not the actual data of any country or institution.

Reproduction

Record the series code, vintage dates, adjustment method and software, model, seasonal factor table, outlier treatment and each revision's release date; recompute with adjusted = raw ÷ seasonal factor, noting that the factor table here is fixed while institutions update seasonal factors in routine recalculations, so this division is an approximation that will not usually reproduce an officially adjusted series; label hypothetical scenarios separately from observations.

Strength of conclusions

The results are descriptive consistency: it is fair to say the December increase is mostly seasonal, and that a monthly change smaller than the revision is not enough to establish a trend. It is not fair to say consumption strengthened, that policy worked, or that any causal link exists.

Revisions are not error corrections: the ONS explains that they improve quality by incorporating additional data, benchmarking, improved methods or rebasing, that first estimates trade off timeliness against accuracy, and that revisions are normally reported in the next release [2].

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "RES-004". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "seasonal adjustment". Column two states the transmission channel of "calendar effects". Column three states the institutional boundary of "data revisions". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "calendar effects" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. data revisions is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Exercises

  1. December raw is 132.0 and the seasonal factor is 1.20. What is the adjusted value?
  2. November adjusted is 110.2 and December adjusted is 109.3. What is the monthly change?
  3. The first release shows 0.0% and the revised figure is -0.8%. How should the conclusion be stated?
Suggested answers
  1. 132.0 ÷ 1.20 = 110.0.
  2. 109.3 ÷ 110.2 - 1 ≈ -0.8%.
  3. Say “the first estimate was 0.0% and the revised figure is -0.8%; the change is no larger than the revision, so it does not establish a trend,” and keep both vintage dates.
