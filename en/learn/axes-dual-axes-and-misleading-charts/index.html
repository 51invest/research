Axes, Dual Axes and Misleading Charts

Axes map values to positions, so changing an axis range or adding a second vertical axis can manufacture crossings and co-movement without touching the data.

ModuleRES-M05 · 图表与表达
LevelFoundation
Read11 min · 13 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

By the end of this article, you can see how much a truncated axis or a dual axis magnifies a “change” or a “crossing,” and redraw the data in one unit so a scale choice is not read as an economic fact.

Research question

Why can the same numbers support opposite conclusions in two charts? Using two hypothetical series, this article tests whether changing the vertical range, or plotting each line against its own axis, moves the crossing point and the visual relationship, and when a crossing carries information. The scope is positional encoding, not errors in the underlying data.

Method and assumptions

A vertical axis maps a value to a position: p = (v - min) ÷ (max - min), so the height per unit is H ÷ (max - min). The same value sits in different places under different ranges, which means slopes and crossings depend on the axis. A dual-axis chart maps the series to two separate scales, so the crossing is set by the data together with both scales, not by the data alone; where the scales differ and overlap, no quantity can be read off the intersection. The examples assume linear axes, a shared horizontal time axis and readers who read the tick labels.

Results

With the left axis at 95–115 and the right axis at 6.5–8.5 (revenue on the left, margin on the right), revenue in January sits at (100 - 95) ÷ 20 = 0.25 and margin at (8.0 - 6.5) ÷ 2 = 0.75; in March both are 0.50, so the lines cross exactly in March.

Move the right axis to 6.5–8.0 and the margin's position rises to 0.67 in March and is 0.53 in April, while revenue is 0.60 in April; the crossing moves to between late March and early April (about month 3.7, reading the straight segment drawn between the March and April points, which adds no data value). The data did not change.

Truncating the axis changes the impression too: revenue rises from 100 to 112, a 12% increase. On a 0–120 axis that change covers 10% of the height; on 95–115 it covers 60%, so the same increase looks six times steeper.

Indexing both series to January = 100 and plotting them on one axis gives revenue 100→112 and margin 100→87.5, one rising and one falling, with no crossing. Indexing compares relative change, not levels.

Figures in plain language

The first two figures show how a truncated axis and a dual axis change what the eye sees; the next two give two alternatives: index different units to a common base, or answer whether the series really must share one frame before drawing a dual axis.

Robustness

A log axis makes equal percentage changes equal heights and stops high bases from looking steeper, but a log axis and a base-of-100 transform both require every value to be positive; rebasing to June = 100 leaves the direction unchanged; splitting the chart into two stacked panels with a shared time axis and one vertical axis each is safer than overlapping scales. Growth rates give revenue +2.0%, +2.9%, +1.9%, +2.8%, +1.8%. The margin is already a percentage, so its "growth rates" of -2.5%, -3.8%, -2.7%, -2.7%, -1.4% are relative changes of that percentage, not percentage-point differences, which readers often misread; percentage-point changes are the safer framing. The two directions are opposite and the magnitudes differ. If a conclusion changes with the axis range, what changed is the picture, not the data. This project's own transformation rule is stricter: docs/03-data-and-technology.md allows a start-of-period value of 100 only for prices and positive-valued indices, forbids the transform when the start is zero or negative, and keeps rate series in raw percentage points, basis-point changes or an explicit standardisation rather than a base-of-100 or growth-of-growth reading. Rebasing the margin to 100 and reading its "growth" above is therefore a demonstration of the misleading device, not a recommended construction here.

The number and width of classes are themselves a choice: OpenStax states that a histogram labels the horizontal axis with what the data represent and the vertical axis with frequency or relative frequency, and that bars or classes must be selected, commonly five to fifteen [3].

Limitations

Reading a chart depends on habit and colour, and this article only covers position and scale. Dual axes are not always wrong: when units differ and both series must be shown, keep them, but label both units, state why the ranges were chosen and avoid reading a crossing as an event. Truncating a line chart can be defensible; truncating a bar chart breaks the length encoding. The numbers are hypothetical, and the analysis says nothing about the chart maker's intent. The confirmed site convention overrides the general practice: verification charts here do not use dual axes — series of different units go into stacked panels sharing one horizontal axis, and only series of the same unit may share a vertical axis or be indexed to a common start (see the preview-chart conventions in HANDOFF). The geometric analysis of dual axes above explains how they manufacture a misreadable crossing; it is not permission to use them in this site's charts.

A histogram splits the data range into equal-sized classes that the user may set arbitrarily or by a rule, with frequency on the vertical axis and the measured variable on the horizontal, which is why it shows centre, spread, skewness, outliers and modes — the NIST/SEMATECH handbook wording [2].

Reproduction

Record the data table, each axis minimum and maximum, any indexing or log transform, and the plotting code and version; recompute key positions with p; report the interval where the lines cross rather than saying only that they cross.

Strength of conclusions

The results are descriptive: the axis choice changes the picture, the crossing follows from the data and the two scales together, and a crossing does not show which series leads, prove causation, or establish correlation. It is fair to say a chart crosses under a stated axis range; it is not fair to say the crossing is an economic event.

A scatter plot places Y on the vertical axis and X on the horizontal to show the relationship between two variables, and no statistical procedure can prove causation from it; the same limit stated in the NIST/SEMATECH handbook applies to the dual-axis charts here [1]. Our judgements about axis mapping, truncated axes, log axes and dual axes are geometric derivations and our own framing. The three sources support the scatter-plot convention and histogram definition and binning; they are not cited as backing for these chart-drawing conclusions.

Knowledge-point review

chart coordinates

Definition: Coordinates map values to positions and scales, using Cartesian axes, time axes and categorical axes.

Mechanism: Axis range, origin and tick spacing determine visual slopes; truncated axes exaggerate or compress changes, and zero and negative handling affect reading.

Example: For example, the same price series can look very different under different axis ranges.

Boundary: Coordinates are not neutral; truncation, non-zero origins and unequal ticks mislead, irregular time axes and point density affect perception, and units and ranges must be labelled.

dual axes

Definition: Dual axes use left and right vertical scales for two quantities of different units or magnitudes on one chart.

Mechanism: They show correlation but scale choice can create or hide relationships; alignment, colour and labels determine readability.

Example: For example, GDP and unemployment with different units can use dual axes, but scaling can make them look synchronised or divergent.

Boundary: Dual axes are not a default; scale manipulation misleads, readers misread crossings, comparisons should standardise or facet, and dual axes need clear labels and caution.

indexing

Definition: Indexing rebases a series to 100 or 1 at a base date for comparing relative changes across different scales.

Mechanism: It preserves growth rates but loses absolute levels; base choice, chaining and year-on-year matter, and series need a common base.

Example: For example, setting two stocks to 100 at the start compares cumulative gains rather than price levels.

Boundary: Indexing is not raw data; base manipulation exaggerates differences, absolute scale and volatility are hidden, and dividends, FX and composition can make indices incomparable.

logarithmic scales

Definition: A logarithmic scale shows proportional rather than absolute differences, with equal distances representing equal percentage changes.

Mechanism: Log axes suit long-run growth, multiple orders of magnitude and heavy tails; zero and negatives cannot be shown, and readers may misread absolute gaps.

Example: For example, 10 to 20 and 100 to 200 are equally spaced on a log axis.

Boundary: Log is not a default; it compresses large values, changes visual slopes, readers may underrate absolute changes, zero and extreme handling is limited, and labelling is essential.

facets and small multiples

Definition: Faceting splits data into small multiples by category on shared or independent scales for comparing patterns without dual axes.

Mechanism: Shared or independent coordinates affect comparability; small multiples suit many categories, trend comparison and avoiding overplotting.

Example: For example, inflation faceted by country reveals common trends and local differences.

Boundary: Faceting is not simple slicing; too many categories fragment, independent scales hide absolute gaps, sparse groups are noisy, and facet choice can hide aggregate patterns.

scatter plots

Definition: A scatter plot shows the joint distribution of observations on two axes, revealing correlation, clusters, outliers and nonlinearity.

Mechanism: Point size, transparency and fitted lines add information; overplotting obscures, and a correlation coefficient does not replace visual pattern.

Example: For example, income versus consumption can show nonlinear saturation and outliers.

Boundary: Scatter is not causal; correlation can come from a third variable, axis truncation and density mislead, and fitted lines can overread noise.

heatmaps

Definition: A heatmap uses colour to represent values in a matrix, common for correlation, geography, time and group comparisons.

Mechanism: Colour scale, ordering and missing values determine reading; clustering reveals patterns but can create visual groups.

Example: For example, a correlation heatmap shows asset co-movement with colour intensity.

Boundary: Heatmaps are not precise reading; colour blindness, printing and scale choice mislead, clustering order can be manipulated, colour is poor for exact comparison, and values and legends are needed.

distribution charts

Definition: Distribution charts show frequencies, densities, quantiles or tails via histograms, box plots, density plots and Q-Q plots.

Mechanism: Box plots show median and quartiles, histograms frequencies, density plots smooth; tails and multimodality need specific checks.

Example: For example, a return histogram shows fat tails and skew, and box plots compare group medians and dispersion.

Boundary: Distribution charts are not just averages; binning, smoothing and axes change shape, box plots hide multimodality, and means can mask tails and subgroups.

misleading charts and accessibility

Definition: Misleading charts distort conclusions through truncated axes, cherry-picking, wrong proportions, dual axes and missing baselines; accessibility makes charts readable for colour-blind, low-vision and device-diverse users.

Mechanism: Distortion comes from axis manipulation, area-value mismatch, selective periods and causal hints; accessibility needs contrast, colour redundancy, alt text and scaling.

Example: For example, a truncated axis makes a tiny difference look huge, and red-green contrast fails colour-blind readers.

Boundary: Charts are not decoration; distortion can be intentional or accidental, accessibility is not an add-on, and colour-only, animated and low-contrast designs exclude users, so design must include it.

Exercises

  1. On an axis of 95–115, what is the position of 107?
  2. With the left axis at 95–115 and the right at 6.5–8.5, in which month do the lines cross?
  3. Why do the lines stop crossing after indexing?
Suggested answers
  1. (107 - 95) ÷ 20 = 0.60.
  2. March: revenue (105 - 95) ÷ 20 = 0.50 and margin (7.5 - 6.5) ÷ 2 = 0.50.
  3. Both series share one unit (January = 100), so positions are comparable, and one series rises while the other falls.
