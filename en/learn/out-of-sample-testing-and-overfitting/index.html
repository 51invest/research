Out-of-Sample Testing and Overfitting

Searching 500 parameter sets over 180 months gives a best in-sample Sharpe of 0.831 that falls to 0.072 out of sample; reusing the out-of-sample window for selection raises the reported value to 1.131, while the same combination falls to 0.489 over the final 60 months.

Research question

Using a simulated 300-month return series, the article searches 500 parameter sets over the first 180 months, treats the middle 60 months as the first out-of-sample window and the last 60 as the final holdout. How much of the best in-sample result survives out of sample? What happens if the analyst reuses the middle 60 months to select a model? The data are generated with seed 20261036 and are not real market data.

Method and assumptions

The strategy uses 500 parameter combinations of one signal (lookback and holding period). The in-sample window selects the combination with the highest Sharpe; the first out-of-sample window is evaluated once. A separate exercise has an analyst select 20 times on the middle 60 months, report the best Sharpe, and test that combination on 60 untouched months. Sharpe is the monthly mean divided by the standard deviation, then multiplied by √12, with no costs deducted. The first out-of-sample window does not enter the first selection; reusing it 20 times turns it into selection data. Overfitting means treating in-sample noise as signal. NIST/SEMATECH notes that a single fit statistic does not prove a model is adequate, while Chicco and Jurman (2022) state that a test set must not be used repeatedly, because doing so causes overfitting and invalidates the results [2].

Results

The best in-sample Sharpe is 0.831, versus 0.072 in the first out-of-sample window; the in-sample top 10 average -0.002 out of sample, and the in-sample/out-of-sample correlation is -0.061. After 20 out-of-sample selections, the best reported Sharpe is 1.131; the same combination scores 0.489 over the final 60 months, a shrinkage of about 57%.

Robustness

  • This run fixes one split, 180/60/60: other splits or seeds change the numbers, while the overestimation from taking the maximum of 500 in-sample values follows from the selection mechanism itself, not from one draw. Stating results for a 150/75/75 split would require extending the script's parameters and running it, which this article does not do.
  • This article deducts no trading costs and reports no turnover by parameter set; directionally, costs accumulate with turnover, so net Sharpe falls once they are deducted and falls most for the shortest holding periods. The quantified version is in the main article Survivorship Bias and Trading Costs (RES-021).

Limitations

One simulated series is not a real asset; the grid, split points and strategy family are arbitrary. Sharpe estimates are shown without standard errors, and the final 60 months could be unusually high or low. Costs, capacity and regime change are omitted. Reusing the same out-of-sample data turns it into in-sample data in practice; a true final holdout is used once. OpenStax notes that extrapolation beyond the observed range is unreliable even when an equation produces a number; the 60-month holdout cannot be extrapolated to longer periods. Out-of-sample evaluation and cross-validation apply only when a budget of untouched data is reserved, when time-series folds are split in time order so that no future information leaks, and when each fold is used for a single evaluation - re-selecting on the same fold turns it into training data. The same source limits their value: a single out-of-sample estimate is itself noisy and is reported here without standard errors, shorter holdouts are less reliable, and performance outside the tested window cannot be inferred from it. The correlation is computed over all 500 combinations (restricting it to the in-sample top ten gives 0.089), so the figure is basis-dependent, while the near-zero relation between in-sample rank and out-of-sample result holds on either basis. The evidence does not support any forecast of a real strategy's return.

Reproduction

  1. Generate 300 monthly returns with seed 20261036.
  2. Enumerate 500 lookback and holding-period combinations.
  3. Select the best Sharpe over the first 180 months and evaluate it on the middle 60.
  4. Simulate 20 out-of-sample selections and record the best Sharpe.
  5. Evaluate the same combination on the final 60 months and report the results.

Strength of conclusions

The best in-sample value is the maximum of 500 comparisons and contains selection bias; it does not carry over out of sample. Reusing the out-of-sample window brings that bias back. The evidence supports "set aside a final sample and evaluate it once"; it does not support treating 0.831 or 1.131 as achievable; the holdout reading of 0.489 is itself one noisy draw.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Out-of-Sample Testing and Overfitting"

The subject of this article is "Out-of-Sample Testing and Overfitting". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "out-of-sample". Column two states the transmission channel of "overfitting". Column three states the institutional boundary of "in-sample". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "overfitting" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. in-sample is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Boundary case: Five hundred parameter sets: in-sample 0.831, out-of-sample 0.072

The first 180 months are used to search 500 parameter sets; the best in-sample Sharpe is 0.831. Over the next 60 months, out-of-sample Sharpe falls to 0.072. Repeated selection on that middle period raises the reported value to 1.131, while the final 60-month holdout is only 0.313. Trying only 20 sets lowers the expected maximum in-sample Sharpe, but selection bias remains; using the out-of-sample period for selection destroys the independence of the holdout.

The conclusion reverses when an economic mechanism supports the result and out-of-sample performance is stable: if parameters are constrained by prior theory, costs are low and several markets agree, 0.072 may not be the final word, but new data are required. The boundary is that in-sample, out-of-sample and holdout are three different gates. Reusing the holdout is like giving students the exam in advance.

These numbers come from the simulation in this article, not strategy performance.

Exercises

  1. Why does the best in-sample Sharpe overstate skill?
  2. What is the point of evaluating out of sample only once?
  3. Why is 1.131 not credible after 20 out-of-sample selections?
  4. Does overfitting occur only when there are many parameters?
Suggested answers
  1. The maximum of 500 noisy estimates tends to select positive noise, so it exceeds true skill.
  2. It keeps the out-of-sample window from participating in selection and preserves a relatively independent test.
  3. 1.131 is the maximum of 20 selections made on the same out-of-sample window; the final holdout gives only 0.489, so the reported figure was inflated by about 57%, while a single holdout is itself a noisy estimate rather than a new constant.
  4. No; repeatedly looking at the same data, changing rules or choosing the sample window can also turn noise into a signal.
