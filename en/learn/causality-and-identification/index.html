Causality and Identification

Causal identification is not a significant regression; it maps observed data to a counterfactual through design or assumptions. Random experiments provide a benchmark, while observational methods each carry their own conditions.

Research question

A hypothetical 12-month experiment with 100 teams: 50 randomly assigned to a research checklist, 50 working as usual. Does the checklist raise monthly excess return? Why does randomization support causation, and why is a comparison by adoption insufficient? Numbers are hypothetical.

Data are hypothetical; the outcome is monthly excess return in percentage points. The figures fit together: adopters are the 42 treated teams that used the checklist plus the 5 control teams that adopted it, i.e. 47 teams, against 53 non-adopters. With adopter and non-adopter means of 1.262% and 0.862% (shown rounded in the table), the weighted overall mean is (47×1.262 + 53×0.862)/100 = 1.05%, the same as the (50×1.30 + 50×0.80)/100 = 1.05% implied by the two arms. The unit of analysis is the team-month, and the 12 months within a team are correlated, so the estimates are clustered by team rather than treated as 1,200 independent observations.

Method and assumptions

Each team has two potential outcomes: Y(1) with the checklist and Y(0) without it. Only one of Y(1) and Y(0) is observed—the fundamental problem. Random assignment makes treatment independent of potential outcomes, so the mean difference identifies the average treatment effect (ATE). Assumptions: random assignment, no interference (SUTVA) and no differential attrition. With noncompliance, ITT identifies the effect of assignment; LATE also requires monotonicity and exclusion. Grouping by adoption creates selection bias; adjustment identifies only a conditional association without ignorability. Experiments change factors and observe responses to study cause and effect (NIST 5.1.1); randomization determines run order (NIST 5.3.3.1) and balances potential differences (OpenStax 1.4).

Results

ITT = 1.30 - 0.80 = 0.50 percentage points. Treating the 1,200 team-month observations as independent gives SE 0.20, t = 2.50 (df = 1,198), p ≈ 0.012 and a 95% interval of 0.11 to 0.89. Clustering by team (100 clusters, df = 99) gives SE 0.28, t = 1.79, p ≈ 0.08 and an interval of -0.06 to 1.06. The point estimate is unchanged, but outcomes in different months of the same team are correlated, so the independent-observation assumption understates the uncertainty and the conventional significance is not robust.

Noncompliance: 8/50 treatment teams did not use the checklist; 5/50 control teams adopted it; the compliance difference is 0.84 - 0.10 = 0.74, so LATE ≈ 0.50 / 0.74 = 0.68 percentage points, under monotonicity and exclusion.

Observational comparison: adopters 1.26%, non-adopters 0.86%, naive difference 0.40; that difference mixes in the selection of who was willing to adopt the checklist and is not the same quantity as the ITT of 0.50. After adjustment it is 0.10 (SE 0.25, interval -0.39 to 0.59). Selection bias can be large and adjustment is not causation.

Robustness

The figures below are also hypothetical; they show which checks to run and cannot be derived from the setting above.

  • Baseline balance: standardized differences in size and past returns are below 0.1; a single randomization need not deliver exact balance.
  • Cluster SE of 0.28 across 100 team clusters; treating team-months as independent overstates precision.
  • Attrition: 6 treatment and 3 control teams are missing during the window; the available-case ITT is 0.46 (SE 0.22); outcome-related attrition still biases.
  • Placebo: the pre-period 12-month return difference is 0.05 (SE 0.18), near zero.
  • A Sharpe-ratio outcome has the same direction; external validity is limited to one institution and a 12-month window.

Limitations

Randomized experiments face small samples, within-team correlation, incomplete compliance, attrition, Hawthorne effects and external validity. Difference-in-differences, instrumental variables and regression discontinuity have their own assumptions and identification is not automatic: difference-in-differences needs the two groups to have moved alike before treatment in the absence of it, so any concurrent change at the treatment date contaminates the difference; an instrument must correlate with the treatment and affect the outcome only through it, and a weak instrument or a breach of the exclusion restriction invalidates the estimate; regression discontinuity identifies only a local effect at the threshold, and it fails if units can manipulate the assignment variable so that the two sides are not comparable. These three summaries are this article's own methodological statement; the three registered sources do not cover them. Significance is not causation; causal claims rest on design assumptions, not p-values. The charts and data snapshots on this site show co-movement and correlation: they are associational evidence and do not by themselves constitute causal evidence. Numbers are hypothetical and cannot be used as strategy advice.

Reproduction

  1. Define treatment, control, outcome and time window.
  2. Check randomization balance and compliance rates.
  3. Compute ITT with standard errors clustered by the assignment unit.
  4. Report noncompliance and attrition; compute LATE where needed and state its assumptions.
  5. Run placebo and subsample checks.
  6. Distinguish ITT, LATE and the observational association.
ITT = mean(Y | assigned=1) - mean(Y | assigned=0)
LATE = ITT / (compliance_treated - compliance_control)

Strength of conclusions

Random assignment supports a causal reading of the 0.50 point ITT, but the clustered interval crosses zero; LATE ≈ 0.68 depends on compliance assumptions. The observational 0.40 falls to 0.10 after adjustment, showing large selection bias. This is ITT evidence for a finite population and a single window, not a universal law.

Exercises

  1. Why does random assignment support causal identification?
  2. What is the difference between ITT and LATE?
  3. What does a larger cluster-robust SE imply?
Suggested answers
  1. Random assignment makes treatment independent of potential outcomes, so group differences cannot reflect pre-treatment selection.
  2. ITT is the assignment effect and includes noncompliance; LATE is the complier effect and requires monotonicity and exclusion.
  3. Outcomes are correlated within teams; ignoring this understates the SE and overstates significance.
