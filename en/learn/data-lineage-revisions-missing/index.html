Problem and conclusion

"Data Lineage: Revisions, Missingness, and Pipeline Audits" looks like a technical detail, but it decides a larger question: are we comparing the same object, or placing numbers from different conventions side by side? This article uses "data lineage" as the entry point, moves through "revision" and "Missing", and ends with a checkable conclusion. The conclusion is not "bigger is better" or "safer is better"; it is that the metric can support comparison only when unit, time and institutional boundary agree.

State the conclusion in three lines: direction, condition and counterexample. If the three lines cannot be written, the concept is not defined yet, and more data will only amplify the confusion.

Definitions and boundaries

"data lineage" has at least three common definitions: a stock, a flow and a ratio. A stock is a balance at a point in time; a flow is a change over a period; a ratio divides one by the other. Change the denominator and the conclusion changes. Treating a stock growth rate as a flow growth rate is one of the most common errors.[1]

"revision" brings the definition into institutions. Who is included and who is excluded depends on law, supervision and accounting rules. Before a cross-country comparison, confirm that the same rule binds the sample; otherwise the observed gap may be an institutional difference, not a behavioural one.[2]

"Missing" determines the boundary. A metric can be valid in a normal range and fail in an extreme range. Dropping extreme observations overstates stability; treating them as normal understates risk.[3]

Mechanism chain

The first chain is "data lineage to revision". A change in the first alters the observable value of the second, but valuation, timing and statistical revision sit in between; one cannot jump straight to a conclusion.

The second chain is "revision to Missing". Institutions turn a metric into an incentive: the same number implies different behaviour under different rules.

The third chain is feedback. Behaviour changes, and the metric itself is redefined or recollected, producing a metric-behaviour-metric loop. Ignoring the loop turns an endogenous change into an exogenous shock.

The fourth chain is the boundary. If any link leaves its valid range, the whole chain must be downgraded to a scenario analysis.

Data and conventions

First align the unit: is the level nominal or real, what is the denominator of the ratio, and which year is the base of the index? Then align time: annual, quarterly and monthly series cannot be spliced without a stated conversion, and seasonal adjustment must be described.[4]

Next run a revision check. Advance, revised and final values can point in different directions; backtesting a decision with final data introduces future information. Finally, recompute independently: switch to another source and confirm that the order of magnitude and direction still agree.

The four checks need no complex model. They need a table with denominator, base period, timing and source for every number. If the table cannot be filled, the number does not belong in the conclusion.

Evidence grades

Evidence about "data lineage" comes in at least four layers. The first is law, regulation and statistical definition: what is counted, what is excluded. The second is a consistent dataset: how large and in which direction. The third is academic or official evaluation: whether the mechanism is identified. The fourth is a case narrative: how it happened under specific conditions.

The layers cannot substitute for one another. Clear rules do not prove a quantity; a quantity does not prove causation; a vivid case does not prove generalisability. Attach every sentence to one layer so the reader knows how much to trust it.

When two layers conflict, check the convention first, then the timing, and only then the explanation. Most conflicts are not "right versus wrong"; they are two numbers answering different questions.

Timeline and institutional dates

"revision" does not change evenly. A rule taking effect, a statistical revision or a change in market structure creates a break. Splicing data across a break into a smooth curve disguises institutional change as a trend.

Handle the timeline with three dates: the rule's effective date, the date the data first became available, and the date of the revision. When the three differ, reconstruct the decision with what was visible then, not with what is known now.

This matters most for "Missing". After an extreme event, supervision and disclosure usually change; using post-event parameters to simulate the pre-event world systematically overstates or understates the shock. The timeline is not decoration; it is part of identification.

Orders of magnitude and sensitivity

Calculate the order of magnitude before the precise value. Move "data lineage" by 10%, 25% and 50%, and see whether the conclusion survives. If a 10% change flips it, the claim depends on a narrow parameter range.

Next test the denominator. The same numerator divided by output, population, assets or transaction volume can produce a different ranking. Then test the sample: drop the top and bottom 5% and see whether the mean, elasticity and correlation remain stable.

Sensitivity analysis is not a collection of scenarios. Its purpose is to identify the assumption the conclusion fears most. Once found, write that assumption as an explicit condition rather than hiding it in a footnote.

Counterfactual and boundaries

A counterfactual is not "what if the event had not happened". It is "what would change if one operable variable moved while everything else stayed fixed". For "revision", the operable variable may be a rule, a price, an inventory or a disclosure frequency; do not change three at once.

A valid counterfactual states what changed, what stayed fixed, which metric measures the result and how long the observation lasts. Miss one and the counterfactual becomes a story.

The boundary is simpler: separate the normal, stress and extreme ranges of "Missing". Normal-range conclusions can guide routine judgement; stress-range conclusions are risk warnings; extreme-range conclusions are scenarios, not baseline forecasts.

Three misreadings

First, treating "data lineage" as a single metric. It may be a composite whose components move in opposite directions; the total then hides the mechanism.

Second, treating the institutional difference in "revision" as a behavioural difference. Different rules create different incentives; without aligning institutions, the comparison is between two worlds.

Third, extrapolating beyond the boundary of "Missing". Correlations, elasticities and fees that hold in a normal range change in a crisis or at an extreme price. Before extrapolating, ask whether the sample covers the case.

Application checklist

  • What are the entity, period and unit of this number?
  • Is the denominator output, population, assets or transaction volume?
  • Are nominal and real, adjusted and unadjusted series aligned?
  • Which entities does the rule cover, and which are excluded?
  • Which assumption, if changed, reverses the conclusion?
  • Can an independent source reproduce the order of magnitude?

The checklist turns a concept into an executable judgement; it does not replace thought. Every item needs an evidence location, or it is only a slogan.

Review and the next judgement

Review is not a restatement of the outcome; it is an audit of the evidence chain. First check whether the convention of "data lineage" was revised after the fact, then whether the institution behind "revision" changed, and finally whether the boundary of "Missing" was crossed.

Write the judgement as a falsifiable sentence: "If A happens, B should appear within C; otherwise the claim fails." Only then can the next similar case distinguish a wrong model from changed conditions.

Keep three records: the sources used, the information unavailable at the time, and the reason the judgement changed afterwards. Separating the three reduces hindsight bias and lets articles in the same module check one another.

One-page worksheet

  • Subject: data lineage, revision, Missing.
  • Convention: unit, denominator, base period, seasonal adjustment.
  • Institution: covered entities, excluded entities, effective date.
  • Quantity: baseline, three sensitivities, the assumption most feared.
  • Evidence: one rule, one dataset, one study, one case.
  • Conclusion: direction, condition and refutation signal in one sentence each.
  • Review: independent source, recomputation, reserved objection.

A full worksheet does not prove the conclusion. It exposes hidden assumptions so the next reader can refute them item by item.

Exercises

  1. Explain "data lineage" in three sentences and give a small case where it does not apply.
  2. Convert "data lineage" to another unit and say how the denominator changes the conclusion.
  3. Write the institutional boundary of "revision" and name two excluded entities.
  4. Use "Missing" to build a counterexample where the normal-range conclusion fails.
  5. Take one number from the article and check its base period and unit at the source.
  6. Turn the four data checks into a table with one example of your own.
  7. If you switch to an independent source, which order of magnitude is most likely to change?
  8. Against another main article in the module, which question does this piece answer better?
  9. Explain the conclusion to a non-specialist in 200 words, including one condition.
  10. List two observable signals that would invalidate the conclusion.

Review record

Record three lines: the last access date of the source, the gap between the independent recomputation and the original result, and the opposing view still unresolved. Each line needs a date and a number; a review without a number is an opinion, not evidence for the next judgement. Set the gap threshold before the review and do not move it afterwards. If the gap exceeds it, return to the four-column worksheet instead of adding "broadly consistent" after the conclusion.
