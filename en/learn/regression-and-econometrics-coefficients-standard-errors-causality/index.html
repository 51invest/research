Regression and Econometrics: Coefficients, Standard Errors and Causality

Regression gives conditional association, not automatic causation. Coefficient size, standard errors, confidence intervals and model specification jointly determine the strength of a conclusion; shrinking standard errors or piling on controls can manufacture false certainty.

Research question

Given observational data, how strong is the relationship between xxx and yyy? Can it be interpreted as xxx causing yyy? Regression answers part of the first question but not automatically the second. This article uses simple regression to explain coefficients, standard errors and causal identification[1].

Data

Paired xxx and yyy observations, sample source, time range and missing-value treatment. For causal interpretation, also exogenous shocks, instruments, natural experiments or panel fixed effects. Frequency and definitions must match, or the coefficient may be a statistical artefact[2]; see the prediction entry for out-of-sample checks[3].

Method

Simple linear regression is:

y=a+bx+ey = a + b x + ey=a+bx+e

The slope estimate is:

with intercept a=yˉ−bxˉa = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}a=yˉ​−bxˉ. The standard error measures sampling uncertainty in bbb; a 95% confidence interval is about b±1.96 SE(b)b \pm 1.96 , SE(b)b±1.96SE(b). R2R^2R2 measures the share of variance explained, not causation.

Results

With x=1,2,3x=1,2,3x=1,2,3 and y=2,4,6y=2,4,6y=2,4,6, slope b=2b=2b=2, intercept a=0a=0a=0, R2=1R^2=1R2=1. The data are perfectly linear, but only 3 points cannot support a population inference. If the standard error is 0.5, the 95% interval is about 1.0–3.0; if 0.1, it is narrower. Report size and uncertainty together.

Robustness

Change the sample period, controls, functional form, remove outliers and use clustered standard errors; check whether signs and significance are stable. If coefficients move a lot with controls, omitted variables or collinearity may be severe. Robustness is not “more regressions” but reasoned changes in assumptions.

Limitations

First, omitted variables; second, reverse causality; third, measurement error; fourth, sample selection; fifth, wrong functional form; sixth, multiple comparisons. Under these conditions, coefficients cannot be read as causal effects. Association in observational data may come from a common cause.

Reproduction

Report sample, variable definitions, model, standard-error type and confidence intervals; provide data and code; check outliers and collinearity; present main results next to alternative specifications. The goal is not a significant coefficient but a readable boundary for the conclusion.

Data and institutional check

Before comparing "Slope, intercept and fit" with "Standard errors and confidence intervals", check four things: whether units and denominators match; whether nominal and real, adjusted and unadjusted series have been reconciled; whether the sample is bound by one legal and accounting regime; and whether an independent source can reproduce the order of magnitude.

Three counterexamples

  1. Counterexample 1. Think of a high R² as a correct model: a high goodness of fit could come from a trend, a lag term, or overfitting. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.
  2. Counterexample 2. Wrong selection of the model based on the lowest standard: A small standard error does not mean clean recognition. It may be that the clustering level is selected incorrectly. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.
  3. Counterexample 3. Interpreting thresholds and interactions with linear regression: when the relationship reverses at both ends of the sample, an average slope obscures the mechanism. Its value is not to reject the whole article but to show where the conclusion starts to need extra evidence.

All three point to the same thing: the model is a conditional statement, not an unconditional forecast. State the conditions and the reader knows when to use it and when to stop.

Deeper note: regressor choice, functional form and robust standard errors

Omitted-variable bias is regression's most common enemy. If the true model is y=a+bx+cz+ey=a+bx+cz+ey=a+bx+cz+e but the researcher regresses yyy on xxx alone, the coefficient on xxx absorbs zzz: bshort=b+c⋅Cov⁡(x,z)Var⁡(x)b_{\text{short}}=b+c\cdot\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x,z)}{\operatorname{Var}(x)}bshort​=b+c⋅Var(x)Cov(x,z)​. The short regression is unbiased only when zzz is uncorrelated with xxx. Treating correlation as causation usually means zzz was left out.

Functional form also changes conclusions. A linear model assumes each extra unit of xxx moves yyy by the same amount; if the true relation is U-shaped or logarithmic, the linear coefficient is only an average slope and may hide a turning point. Adding squares, interactions or segments requires data distribution and economic meaning, not just R2R^2R2.

Standard errors are easier to get wrong than coefficients. Classical standard errors assume homoskedastic, independent errors; if variance changes with xxx (heteroskedasticity), standard errors are too small and t-statistics inflated; if observations are correlated within industries, regions or time, cluster them. Panel data need individual and serial correlation treatment. Always state the standard-error type and clustering level.

Multicollinearity does not bias coefficients but inflates standard errors: two highly correlated regressors may both be insignificant individually while the joint test is significant. Judge it with variance inflation factors and economic overlap, not a single t-statistic.

R2R^2R2 is not the whole of model quality. Adding variables always raises R2R^2R2, so use adjusted R2R^2R2, information criteria and out-of-sample prediction. Good in-sample fit with poor out-of-sample performance is overfitting; economic research also asks whether coefficients match theory and survive different samples and specifications[2][3].

Sample size and statistical power determine whether a real effect can be detected. If the effect is small and the sample has only dozens of observations, even a real effect may be insignificant; conversely, a large sample turns economically trivial differences into 'significant' ones. Report confidence intervals and economic magnitude alongside p-values. Testing many coefficients also raises the multiple-testing issue[3].

Outliers and missing values can dominate small-sample regressions. One extreme point can tilt the slope; dropping it needs an ex ante rule, not 'the result looked bad'. If missingness is not random, complete-case regression introduces selection bias; state the missingness mechanism and treatment.

Robustness requires several specifications: different functional forms, sample periods, standard errors, and control sets. A conclusion that holds only under one specification is fragile and should be labelled as such, not treated as fact. A robustness table carries more information than a single regression.

Reporting standard: state sample size, coefficients, standard-error type, confidence intervals, functional form and missing-value treatment, so readers can judge the strength of the conclusion rather than a single star. When using clustered standard errors, also report the number of clusters and observations per cluster. This is basic to reproducible research; otherwise readers cannot verify the result.

Evidence grades: who can check this claim

For "Slope, intercept and fit", the source can usually support what it is, who publishes it and how it is measured. It cannot automatically support "the same happens in another market". Pull out the third-layer sentences and the reader can see where the risk sits.

For replication, give one person the raw numbers and assumptions and ask them to reach the same order of magnitude. If they cannot, the article is missing a step, not the reader.

Orders of magnitude: rule out arithmetic error first

Before the mechanism, run three checks. First, convert ratios into absolute amounts: 5% of 1,000 and 5% of a trillion are different objects. Second, convert annualised figures into period values: multiplying a quarterly number by four amplifies seasonality and one-offs. Third, convert nominal into real: high inflation can hide a real contraction behind nominal growth.

None of these needs a complex model, only paper and a calculator. They block the common failure of a plausible story with a wrong order of magnitude. Teaching numbers in the article are labelled assumptions; formal citation follows the date, unit and footnote of the source.

Compress the conclusion into three lines

Line one is the conclusion: one sentence with the entity, direction and condition. Line two is the condition: which assumption must hold. Line three is the counterevidence: what data would make you abandon it.

If the three lines cannot be written, the article is still wrapped in background, significance and implications rather than a testable judgement. Hand it to a reader who disagrees and ask them to attack only lines two and three; that is more useful than asking them to repeat the whole text.

This is the house style: checkable facts first, conditional judgement second, and the unknown left on the table rather than covered by a longer explanation.
