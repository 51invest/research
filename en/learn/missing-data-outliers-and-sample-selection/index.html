Missing Data, Outliers and Sample Selection

The same average growth rate changes with who is sampled, who responds, how missing values are filled and how outliers are treated; reporting the rule and a sensitivity range is more honest than reporting one precise number.

Research question

A survey samples 100 firms from a population of 1,000; 80 respond. The respondent mean growth rate is 6.0%, and the non-respondent mean is assumed to be -2.0%. Looking only at respondents gives 6.0%; weighting 80% and 20% gives 4.4%, a difference of 1.6 percentage points. The question is how missing data, outliers and sample selection change a descriptive statistic, and what to report so readers can judge the evidence. All numbers are hypothetical, and non-respondent values are unobserved in practice.

Data

Suppose an annual firm-growth survey with a target population of 1,000 firms and a sample of 100.

Two smaller examples: five firms with growth rates 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 5.0 and 60.0 (%); ten firms with a profit margin, eight observed values averaging 6.0% and two missing.

An outlier is an observation that lies abnormally far from the other observations in a sample; it may arise from coding error or from genuine variation, and it may only be discarded once an error is confirmed — the NIST/SEMATECH handbook definition [1].

Method and assumptions

Define the population, frame, design and weights; probability sampling gives each unit a known selection chance, and weights scale the sample to the population. Non-response is either unit-level (whole case) or item-level (one variable). Complete-case analysis keeps cases with no missing values; it is simple but can change sample composition. Applied to a time series or a paired panel, it keeps only the periods every series has and drops the other periods outright instead of interpolating them; the dropped periods must be recorded and shown as a gap in the chart, and the cost is that usable observations from the other series in those periods are discarded too while the comparable window shortens. Weighting adjusts the respondent mix using observed characteristics; imputation fills missing values from similar units or a model, adding assumptions. An outlier deviates markedly; check for coding, unit and merge errors first, then decide whether the value is genuine, and set the identification rule before looking at the results instead of choosing it afterwards. Grubbs, ESD and box plots help identify outliers; masking and swamping make a single test miss a real outlier or flag a normal point, so tests should be combined with graphics. Do not delete outliers automatically; use the median, trimmed mean or robust regression, and report results with and without those observations. The key assumption: respondents are comparable with non-respondents given covariates; if missingness depends on the unobserved outcome, weighting and imputation cannot guarantee unbiasedness.

One in-house case is checkable against the same repository: two multi-series monthly panels in this project (both in the project chart register) use complete-case handling, dropping 2025-10 outright because the source has no value. CHART-002 spans 2024-01 to 2026-09 (33 months), leaving 32 observations; CHART-010 spans 2024-01 to 2025-12 (24 months), leaving 23 (see the entries in docs/chart-register.json). This is not an imputation question: dropping the period preserves the period-by-period alignment but shortens the common window and leaves a gap in the chart, so the excluded period has to be recorded in the notes and the figure caption, not merely visible in the plot.

For missing values caused by non-response the ONS imputes, using responses from similar businesses, and states that imputation is preferable to dropping every case with a missing value [2].

Results

In the survey example, the respondent mean is 6.0%; weighting gives 0.8 × 6.0 + 0.2 × (-2.0) = 4.4%, a difference of 1.6 percentage points.

In the five-firm example, mean growth is (4.0 + 5.0 + 6.0 + 5.0 + 60.0) ÷ 5 = 16.0%, the median is 5.0%, and the mean without 60.0% is 5.0%; one observation raises the mean by 11.0 percentage points while the median is unchanged. In the ten-firm example, the eight observed values average 6.0%; if both missing values are 0.0%, the full mean is (8 × 6.0 + 2 × 0.0) ÷ 10 = 4.8%; if both are 12.0%, it is (8 × 6.0 + 2 × 12.0) ÷ 10 = 7.2%. The complete-case 6.0% is only one scenario: the full mean (48.0 + v1 + v2) ÷ 10 rises monotonically with both missing values, so as long as each of them lies between 0.0% and 12.0% the mean must fall between 4.8% and 7.2%, and that range lapses as soon as the assumption changes. A fair report gives the range together with the assumption behind it rather than the single 6.0%.

Robustness

Replace the mean with the median, a trimmed mean or robust regression; use sampling, response or post-stratification weights; compare respondents with the frame on size, industry and region; use best/worst-case scenarios and multiple imputation; change the outlier rule and report results with and without extreme observations; recompute by subgroup. If a claim holds only under one assumption, say so. Failure conditions: missingness related to the unobserved outcome, weights from a wrong frame, a genuine important outlier, and a sample too small for a stable median.

Limitations

Non-respondents' true values are unobserved; weighting adjusts only observed characteristics, and a wrong imputation model gives wrong filled values. An outlier may be a data error or a real structural change, and a statistical rule alone cannot decide. Medians and trimmed means are unstable in small samples. The numbers are hypothetical, not estimates for any survey or population.

Convenience sampling uses results that are readily available and can be highly biased; sampling bias arises when some members of the population are less likely to be selected, which leads to wrong conclusions about the population — the OpenStax framing used in the sample-selection discussion here [3].

Reproduction

Record the frame version, design, field dates, response status, weights, missingness indicators, including any period dropped outright, outlier rules, imputation model and software version; provide a missingness table, an outlier log and a sensitivity range; recompute 6.0%, 4.4%, 16.0%, 5.0%, 4.8% and 7.2% from the raw data, and label hypothetical scenarios.

Strength of conclusions

These are descriptive sensitivity results: the respondent mean is 6.0%, the weighted estimate is 4.4%, one outlier raises the mean by 11.0 percentage points, and, under the assumption that both missing values lie between 0.0% and 12.0%, the full-sample mean falls between 4.8% and 7.2%. It is not fair to say the population growth rate is 4.4%, or that a cause explains the difference.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Missing Data, Outliers and Sample Selection"

The subject of this article is "Missing Data, Outliers and Sample Selection". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "missing data". Column two states the transmission channel of "outliers". Column three states the institutional boundary of "sample selection". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "outliers" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. sample selection is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Exercises

  1. Of 100 firms, 80 respond; the respondent mean is 6.0% and the non-respondent mean is assumed to be -2.0%. What is the weighted mean?
  2. Five firms have growth rates 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 5.0 and 60.0 (%). What are the mean, median and mean without 60.0?
  3. Eight of ten firms have an observed mean of 6.0% and two are missing. What is the full-sample mean if the missing values are 0.0% or 12.0%?
Suggested answers
  1. 0.8 × 6.0 + 0.2 × (-2.0) = 4.4%.
  2. Mean 80.0 ÷ 5 = 16.0%; median 5.0%; without 60.0, 20.0 ÷ 4 = 5.0%.
  3. At 0.0%: (8 × 6.0 + 2 × 0.0) ÷ 10 = 4.8%; at 12.0%: (8 × 6.0 + 2 × 12.0) ÷ 10 = 7.2%.
