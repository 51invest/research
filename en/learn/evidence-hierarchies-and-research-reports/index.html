Evidence Hierarchies and Research Reports

Sampling 12 simulated studies by evidence tier gives realised average effects of 2.4% in tier A, 4.5% in B and 8.2% in C, 4.7% overall and 3.6% excluding the practitioner notes; a report should give tiers, a range and a certainty rating, not one headline number.

Research question

Suppose a research report evaluates the claim that "factor X predicts returns" and has only 12 simulated studies. After grouping them by design, sample coverage and out-of-sample testing, how far does the average effect fall? How should the report present the evidence grade without hiding the tiers behind one average? The data are generated in-repo by scripts/simulate-evidence-hierarchy.mjs with seed 20261038 and are not real studies.

Method and assumptions

Studies are placed in three tiers: A is peer-reviewed, covers the full sample and includes an out-of-sample test; B is peer-reviewed but reports only in-sample results; C is a practitioner note with an incomplete sample definition. The in-sample and out-of-sample terms follow the main article Out-of-Sample Testing and Overfitting (RES-022) and are not redefined here. Each study's effect is a realised draw from a normal distribution with that tier's parameters: the set means are A 2.1%, B 4.8% and C 9.6%, with set standard deviations of A 1.5%, B 2.0% and C 3.0%. The tier averages in the table above are the realised values of these 12 draws (A 2.4%, B 4.5%, C 8.2%), not the parameters, and the difference is exactly the sampling noise of a tier holding only three to five studies. Tier and pooled averages are computed.

That evidence grades should not be a mechanical ranking by study type is this article's own judgement. The GRADE working group homepage states verbatim: "Explicit consideration should be given to each of the GRADE domains for assessing the certainty in the evidence (i.e., risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, publication bias, large effects, dose response gradients, and residual plausible opposing bias)" [3], and the same page calls its method "a common, sensible and transparent approach to grading quality (or certainty) of evidence and strength of recommendations" [3] - eight domains are listed, of which this article uses five, a choice that is ours. On data quality, the IMF DQAF page states verbatim: "Assessments have a six-part structure starting with a review of the legal and institutional environment (prerequisites of quality) and followed by an analysis of five dimensions of quality.", and the five dimensions are listed there as Assurances of integrity, Methodological soundness, Accuracy and reliability, Serviceability, Accessibility [1]. Sackett et al. (1996) are commonly cited for the point that evidence-based medicine is not restricted to randomised trials and meta-analyses [2]; this site could not retrieve that page in this environment, so the sentence is kept as a bibliographic pointer and not as verbatim support (see Limitations).

Results

The 12 studies pool to an average effect of 4.7%; the A+B tier averages 3.6%; tier A alone averages 2.4%. The three C-tier studies are 7.76%, 9.65% and 7.20%, averaging 8.2%, and they are what pulls the pooled figure from tier A's 2.4% up to 4.7%. Changing the inclusion rule from tier A only to all studies moves the pooled estimate across 2.4%-4.7%. Reporting only 4.7% hides the tier differences; reporting only 2.4% ignores the five B-tier studies. Recomputing from the rounded tier averages in the table: (4×2.4 + 5×4.5 + 3×8.2) ÷ 12 = 4.725%, and (4×2.4 + 5×4.5) ÷ 9 = 3.567%, which match the script's 4.7% and 3.6%.

Robustness

  • With size weighting, if large studies are concentrated in tier A, the pooled effect moves closer to 2.4%.
  • Dropping the largest C-tier study (9.65%) lowers the C average to 7.5% and the all-study average to 4.28%.
  • Changing one tier-A out-of-sample result to insignificant lowers the A average, showing quality differences within a tier.
  • Changing the tier definitions (for example, "out-of-sample" to "independent data") changes membership, but the direction that lower-quality evidence shows larger effects remains: over 2000 replications the realised ordering C > B > A holds in 98.5% of runs.
  • Another seed changes the realised values (a tier average can land above or below its set mean) but not the tiering logic; this article publishes the single realisation under seed 20261038.

Assessing certainty should consider the domains quoted above from the GRADE homepage, including risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness and publication bias [3].

Limitations

The 12 studies are a simulation with simplified tiers, and tier means are point estimates without confidence intervals; a real systematic review needs a search strategy, inclusion criteria, effect-size weighting, heterogeneity analysis and publication-bias tests. Tier A can still be biased, and tier C can contain useful field information. An evidence grade is a communication tool, not a truth label. No independent review was performed, so this is not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Of the three sources, the GRADE and IMF pages could be checked; the Sackett et al. (1996) text could not be opened in this pass, so it is a bibliographic pointer only and is not quoted verbatim.

Reproduction

  1. Generate 12 studies with seed 20261038: four in tier A, five in tier B, three in tier C, each study's effect drawn from a normal with that tier's set mean and standard deviation.
  2. Compute tier and pooled averages.
  3. Run the sensitivity check that drops the largest C-tier study.
  4. Fill in an evidence table using the DQAF and GRADE dimensions.
  5. Report tiers, ranges, limitations, sources and review status (who reviewed, and how far), not a single number.

Strength of conclusions

The average effect falls as the evidence tier rises, so the pooled number depends on which studies are included. This does not mean lower-tier studies are necessarily wrong; it means a report must show tiers, ranges and certainty. The evidence supports using evidence grades and data-quality checklists in research reports; it does not support presenting one 4.7% headline as the whole evidence base. Stated by conclusion type: the figures here are a demonstration at the level of observation and method only. They are neither correlational evidence that "factor X predicts returns" nor causal evidence. The design and assumptions a causal claim needs are set out in the main article Causality and Identification (RES-015), and the false positives created by repeated attempts in Multiple Testing and Data Snooping (RES-023). The same standard applies to our own charts: the verification notes for co-movement charts such as CHART-004 and CHART-008 state that contemporaneous co-movement is not causation and cannot support a directional claim, so citing them supports at most "these two series move together" and never a causal statement.

Knowledge-point review

research question

Definition: A research question is an answerable, bounded question with theoretical or practical meaning, specifying object, time, mechanism and observable implications.

Mechanism: Good questions are specific, testable and not too broad; they drive data, design and scope, while vague questions invite data dredging.

Example: For example, “does a minimum-wage rise reduce low-skill employment” is more researchable than “is the minimum wage good”.

Boundary: A question is not a thesis; preset answers, value judgments and unobservable concepts make it untestable, overly narrow questions lack meaning, and literature and decisions must connect.

literature review

Definition: A literature review systematically reads, compares and synthesises prior research to identify consensus, disputes, methods and gaps.

Mechanism: It should cover key theories, evidence, methods and data and state search scope and appraisal criteria; gaps must be specific, not “no one studied it”.

Example: For example, comparing minimum-wage studies across countries, periods and identification methods reveals institutional differences.

Boundary: Literature is not citation stacking; selective citation, English bias and publication bias mislead, reviews can copy errors, and critical appraisal matters more than listing.

research design

Definition: Research design turns a question into a testable plan covering data, sample, identification, variables, comparison and pre-analysis.

Mechanism: Design addresses bias, confounding, power and ethics; experiments, quasi-experiments, observation and simulation have different assumptions, and preregistration limits post hoc changes.

Example: For example, difference-in-differences evaluates a policy with pre-defined treated, control, outcome and window.

Boundary: Design is not method stacking; failed identification, measurement error and small samples invalidate results, complex designs cannot replace a clear question, and post hoc changes bias.

reproducibility

Definition: Reproducibility obtains the same results with the same data, code and methods, or validates conclusions in new data.

Mechanism: It needs accessible data, versioned code, environment, seeds and documentation; computational and empirical reproduction differ.

Example: For example, publishing cleaning and regression code lets others obtain the same coefficients.

Boundary: Reproducibility is not correctness; unavailable data, missing code and confidentiality block it, the same error can reproduce, and independent replication and robustness matter more.

levels of evidence

Definition: Levels of evidence rank designs by bias control and generalisability, from systematic reviews and randomised trials to quasi-experiments, observational studies and cases.

Mechanism: They trade internal and external validity; trials identify strongly but may be narrow, while observational studies are realistic but confounded.

Example: For example, trials support drug causality, systematic reviews synthesise studies, and cases show mechanisms but generalise poorly.

Boundary: Levels are not absolute rankings; a good observational study can beat a bad trial, hierarchy depends on the question, and mechanical use ignores context and measurement.

limitations and reporting

Definition: Limitations state what a study can and cannot claim, and reporting transparently presents question, methods, results, uncertainty and limits.

Mechanism: Boundaries include sample, period, identification assumptions, measurement error and generalisation; reporting should avoid selective results, causal overreach and hidden conflicts.

Example: For example, a single-country, single-period study cannot generalise to all countries, and intervals and limits should be reported.

Boundary: Limitations are not disclaimers; post hoc limits, selective reporting and p-hacking mislead, transparency cannot fix bad design, and conclusions must match evidence strength.

Data and institutional check: evidence hierarchy

Before using "evidence hierarchy" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns research report.

Third, the institutional boundary. certainty of evidence is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: evidence hierarchy

Learning "evidence hierarchy" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "research report": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "certainty of evidence" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of evidence hierarchy still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind research report reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "evidence hierarchy" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "research report" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Decision table: research question, literature review, research design

Check Question Failure signal
research question Can the concept be tied to an observable proxy rather than a label? The proxy tracks the name, not behaviour
literature review Can the mechanism be tested independently, with a counterexample? Only a story remains, with nothing that could refute it
