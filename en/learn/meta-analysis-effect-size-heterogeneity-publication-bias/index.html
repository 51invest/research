Meta-Analysis and Research Synthesis: Effect Size, Heterogeneity and Publication Bias

Meta-analysis does not count papers; it weights comparable effect sizes by precision and reports heterogeneity, bias and certainty of evidence.

The question and its scope

Meta-analysis converts the results of several studies into a common effect size, weights them by precision, and estimates whether the differences among studies exceed random error. It answers not "how many papers support this claim" but "given comparable conditions, how large is the effect, how certain is it, and where does it change?"

This article covers five steps: effect size, weights, heterogeneity, publication bias and certainty of evidence. It does not replace a design review of each study, and it does not merge observational studies and randomised trials into one evidence tier. The numbers below are teaching examples, not a re-computation of any particular review.

Intuition: five papers are not five verdicts

Suppose five studies ask whether a training programme raises earnings, with samples ranging from 120 to 2,000 people. A small sample gives a noisy estimate; a large sample gives a more precise one. Averaging the five effect sizes directly would give a 120-person study the same voice as a 2,000-person study. The first lesson of meta-analysis is to weight by precision, not by the number of papers.

The second lesson is to separate "the same question" from "the same label". All five studies may say "training", but one is a six-week skills course, one a two-year apprenticeship, and one a job-search subsidy. Forcing them into one number produces a precise-looking average of different mechanisms. Before pooling, judge comparability: outcome, comparator, time window and population.

The third lesson is to admit differences. Even if every study is unbiased, the true effect may vary with institutions, dose and implementation quality. Meta-analysis therefore reports both an average effect and how much studies differ. An average without a measure of dispersion hides heterogeneity inside a tidy decimal.

When τ2\tau^2τ2 is large, small studies lose weight and the pooled estimate moves toward the larger studies. This is why fixed and random effects often disagree. Heterogeneity is often summarised by I2I^2I2, the share of total variation attributed to between-study variance. An I2I^2I2 above 50% deserves explanation, not just a footnote.

Publication bias is usually inspected with a funnel plot: effect size on the horizontal axis, standard error on the vertical. Studies should be roughly symmetric. Egger's test regresses asymmetry, but it flags possible bias rather than proving it. Trim-and-fill adds hypothetical symmetric studies to estimate a bias-adjusted effect; the filled studies are not observations and cannot be treated as new evidence.

When pooling is legitimate

First, construct comparability. All studies measure the same outcome in the same direction. Pooling "income" with "employment" is substitution, not synthesis.

Second, comparator comparability. The treatment-control contrast must map to the same mechanism. Mixing "no training" with "another training" changes what the effect means.

Third, independent studies. Two papers from the same sample are not two independent pieces of evidence. Treating them as independent double-counts a large sample and distorts weights.

Fourth, enough reporting. Each study needs an effect size and standard error, or statistics that can be converted. "Significant" without a point estimate cannot enter a pool.

Fifth, bias assessment. At least check publication bias, selective outcome reporting and small-study effects. A symmetric funnel plot is not proof of no bias.

Sixth, no causal leap. Meta-analysis pools estimates from existing designs. If the primary studies are mostly correlational, the pooled result remains correlational; a larger sample does not create identification.

Removing E lowers the fixed estimate to about 0.20 and the random estimate to about 0.28. A 120-person study can move the average by 0.08 to 0.15, which is why small-study effects are a sensitivity issue. If the funnel plot puts small studies on the right, publication bias belongs in the conclusion boundary, not in a footnote.

Choosing between the two models

A fixed-effect model assumes that every study estimates the same parameter; a random-effects model assumes real differences among studies. When studies are close to homogeneous and the goal is to describe this set, fixed effects can be appropriate. When studies come from different populations, doses or institutions and the conclusion is meant to travel, random effects are usually more defensible. The safest practice is to report both and explain whether the difference comes from weights or from τ2\tau^2τ2. If the two models point in opposite directions, do not pick the more attractive one; write the disagreement into the conclusion boundary.

Common misreadings

Misreading one: meta-analysis is a vote count. Five papers in favour and two against do not imply an effect of five-sevenths; precision and design differ, and bias must be assessed.

Misreading two: I2=0I^2=0I2=0 means no problem. I2I^2I2 is a ratio of variances and can be underestimated when samples or events are few. Low heterogeneity does not make every study unbiased.

Misreading three: the pooled estimate is the "true effect". It is a weighted average of a particular set of studies. A different set, outcome or time window will produce a different number.

Misreading four: observational studies and trials can be pooled freely. They answer different questions under different identification assumptions. Report them side by side; do not hide the design difference inside one average.

Misreading five: trim-and-fill "corrects" publication bias. It fills hypothetical studies under a symmetry assumption. It is a sensitivity analysis, not recovered data.

Misreading six: a large effect size means a good policy. SMD is unit-free and relative. A 0.2 versus 0.5 difference must be interpreted against the outcome and the cost; statistical significance is not practical importance.

Three sources of heterogeneity

The first is clinical or policy heterogeneity: populations, doses and implementation differ, so the true effects differ. The second is method heterogeneity: different comparators, outcome measures and follow-up lengths estimate different parameters. The third is reporting heterogeneity: only favourable or significant results are visible, so the observed set of studies is selected.

Heterogeneity is not removed by ignoring it. Start with prespecified subgroup analysis by population, dose or institution; then use meta-regression with study-level covariates; finally run sensitivity analyses that drop high-risk studies one at a time. Subgroups must be pre-specified. Choosing groups after seeing the result is data snooping.

Certainty of evidence: five downgrades in GRADE

GRADE rates certainty as high, moderate, low or very low. Randomised trials start high and observational studies start low. Five factors downgrade: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. Large effects, dose-response, opposing confounding and residual confounding can upgrade.

The point is not to score papers but to state how certain the answer is. A pooled estimate can be precise while certainty is low; a single trial can be imprecise but well designed. Readers need the point estimate, the interval and the certainty together, not a row of stars.

The table prevents "putting different conventions into one formula". If one study reports an odds ratio and another a risk difference, convert them or pool them separately; do not add them directly.

Boundaries and rechecking

Four boundaries constrain the conclusion. The study-set boundary: only published, language-specific, time-specific literature is included, so the result cannot be extrapolated to unrepresented populations. The construct boundary: SMD is unit-free and cannot be converted directly into "how much money people earn". The design boundary: pooling observational studies does not create causal identification. The time boundary: after the policy environment changes, an old average may no longer apply.

When rechecking, read the forest plot first, then the funnel plot, then sensitivity analyses, then GRADE. The order matters: a pooled diamond can look convincing before you see what it is made of. Start with the individual studies and bias, then decide whether the summary is usable.
