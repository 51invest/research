Trends, Nonstationarity and Spurious Regression

Two nonstationary series with their own trends can produce a high R-squared and a significant slope even when they are independent; level-regression significance must be read alongside trends, autocorrelation and differenced results.

Research question

Two hypothetical 120-month independent random walks: A drifts 0.30 per month with shock SD 1.00; B drifts 0.25 per month with shock SD 1.20. Will a level regression of B on A show a high R² and a significant slope? Why? This article explains trends, nonstationarity and spurious regression, contrasting differenced and detrended diagnostics. Every number comes from scripts/simulate-spurious-regression.mjs: the documented draw uses seed 20261030, and the mechanism is reported over 500 independent draws starting from that seed.

A and B are dimensionless teaching series; drift and shock SD are per month, and a slope reads as “the change in B per one-unit change in A”.

Method and assumptions

The level regression B_t = a + b A_t + e_t assumes stationary, independent, homoskedastic errors. With stochastic trends (unit roots), conventional t distributions do not apply; independent trending series can produce falsely significant relationships—spurious regression (Granger & Newbold 1974). Diagnosis includes time plots, autocorrelation, unit-root tests, and comparisons of levels, differences and detrended results. Differences, ΔB_t = a + b ΔA_t + e_t, remove stochastic trends; levels may be meaningful only under cointegration. Autocorrelation detects non-randomness; if randomness fails, use a time-series model (NIST 1.3.5.12).

Results

Start with the documented draw (seed 20261030): levels give b = -0.069, SE = 0.120, t = -0.6, R² = 0.003, p = 0.57, residual lag-1 autocorrelation 0.963. This pair of random walks produced no impressive spurious fit at all - which is exactly why one draw is not evidence. The mechanism lives in the distribution over 500 independent draws: level R² has median 0.793, a 5th percentile of 0.226 and a 95th percentile of 0.938, and 83% of the draws exceed 0.5. The series are independent by construction; the fit comes from their separate random trends drifting in the same direction in a finite sample, not from an economic link.

Across the same 500 draws, difference R² has median 0.004 and a 95th percentile of 0.031, with no draw above 0.5; in the documented draw, differences give b = 0.052, SE = 0.124, t = 0.4, R² = 0.001, p = 0.68, correlation 0.04. Differencing removes the unit root, so the spurious relation disappears. Detrending behaves differently: the documented draw gives b = -0.945, SE = 0.087, t = -10.9, R² = 0.500, p = 0.00, and over 500 draws the median is 0.091 with a 95th percentile of 0.491 and 5% of draws above 0.5. Subtracting a fitted straight line from a random walk leaves a unit root behind - detrending does not remove the unit root - which is precisely the dividing line against trend-stationary series.

Robustness

  • With another seed or sample window the level R² is usually high again (83% of 500 draws exceed 0.5) while no differenced draw exceeds 0.5; the documented draw's level R² of 0.003 shows that a single seed proves nothing and must not be chosen for how it looks.
  • Log differences (growth rates) in the documented draw: b = 0.245, SE = 0.093, R² = 0.057 - the sign and size of a single draw are unstable, so report the distribution rather than a picked seed.
  • Putting a time trend directly into the level regression is the same operation as detrending here: after detrending a random walk still has a unit root, so the median R² over 500 draws is 0.091 with a 95th percentile of 0.491 rather than an automatically clean result.
  • If the series are trend-stationary rather than unit-root, detrending may restore a relationship; misclassification leads to over-differencing.
  • Unit-root tests have low power; use economic meaning and multiple diagnostics.

Limitations

The simulation illustrates a mechanism, not a real-data test. Real data have breaks, seasonality, measurement error and common shocks; spurious and genuine trends are hard to distinguish. The charts and data snapshots on this site show co-movement and correlation: they are associational evidence, the caution drawn here applies to them, and no causal or forecasting conclusion follows from them. A high R² is not evidence of a correct model; residual analysis informs adequacy (NIST 4.4.4). Unit-root or cointegration tests, robust SEs and out-of-sample validation are needed. Numbers are simulated, not real data.

Strength of conclusions

Mechanically, level R² has a median of 0.793 across 500 independent draws - 83% exceed 0.5 - even though the walks are independent by construction, and differencing collapses the median to 0.004 with no draw above 0.5. The documented draw inverts the easy story: its level R² is only 0.003 while the detrended R² reaches 0.500, which shows both that a single seed is not evidence and that detrending does not remove the unit root. Level significance alone is therefore not evidence of a relationship; trends and nonstationarity must be handled, and results reported as a distribution. This is not a causal or forecasting claim about real markets.

Knowledge-point review

trend stationarity

Definition: Trend stationarity means a series fluctuates around a deterministic trend and is stationary after detrending, unlike a unit-root process where shocks persist.

Mechanism: Trend-stationary series can be detrended, unit roots need differencing; wrong handling causes spurious correlation or overdifferencing.

Example: For example, real GDP is modelled as trend-stationary or unit-root, and the choice affects forecasts and causality.

Boundary: Trend stationarity is not a visual judgment; unit-root tests have low power, structural breaks mislead, trends and random walks are hard to distinguish, and model choice affects long forecasts.

differencing

Definition: Differencing subtracts the previous value from the current one to remove trend and unit roots and make a series more stationary.

Mechanism: First differences give changes, seasonal differencing removes seasonal patterns; overdifferencing introduces negative autocorrelation and noise.

Example: For example, differencing price levels gives returns and differencing GDP gives growth.

Boundary: More differencing is not better; it loses level information, amplifies noise, treats unit roots and trend stationarity differently, and changes model interpretation.

lags

Definition: Lags use past values of variables as regressors or instruments to capture dynamics, expectations and timing.

Mechanism: Lag order follows information criteria, theory and sample; lags address autocorrelation and reverse causality but can add omission and overfitting.

Example: For example, past quarterly investment forecasts current GDP, or lagged policy identifies effects.

Boundary: Lags are not causal proof; selection, look-ahead and lag-length manipulation matter, lagged variables can correlate with other factors, and dynamic models need stability checks.

cointegration

Definition: Cointegration means a linear combination of non-stationary series is stationary, indicating a long-run equilibrium.

Mechanism: Engle-Granger or Johansen tests identify it, and error-correction models capture long-run relation and short-run adjustment.

Example: For example, house prices and income may trend but a price-to-rent or income ratio can be in long-run equilibrium.

Boundary: Cointegration is not any correlation; spurious regression, breaks and sample periods create false cointegration, the relation can shift, and strategies need adjustment speed and costs.

seasonality

Definition: Seasonality is a pattern repeating at fixed intervals such as months, quarters or weeks, driven by weather, holidays, production and behaviour.

Mechanism: Seasonal models use dummies, Fourier terms, seasonal differencing or decomposition; adjustment and forecasting must separate true seasonality from one-offs.

Example: For example, retail rises before Spring Festival and electricity peaks in summer.

Boundary: Seasonality is not fixed; moving holidays, climate and structure change patterns, over-adjustment removes real signal, and seasonal and cyclical effects are easily confused.

rolling analysis

Definition: Rolling analysis re-estimates or backtests over moving windows to see how parameters, returns and risk change.

Mechanism: Window length trades stability for adaptability; rolling backtests detect overfitting and regime shifts but create multiple comparisons and boundary effects.

Example: For example, a 36-month rolling window estimates beta and shows drift.

Boundary: Rolling is not out-of-sample proof; window choice affects results, overlapping windows correlate, rolling backtests can still snoop, and breaks invalidate historical windows.

structural change and volatility

Definition: Structural change is a persistent shift in the data-generating process at some point, and volatility is variance clustering and changing over time.

Mechanism: Chow, Bai-Perron and ARCH/GARCH models identify them; policy, technology and crises often create breaks.

Example: For example, volatility rose and clustered after the financial crisis, and regulatory reform changed market structure.

Boundary: Structural change is not an outlier; break location and number are uncertain, volatility clustering can have confounders, and historical windows no longer represent the future after breaks.

Exercises

  1. Why do two independent trending series produce a significant slope, and why is detrending not a cure?
  2. What is the difference between differencing and detrending?
  3. Why is a high R² not proof?
Suggested answers
  1. Both rise over time, so the regression mistakes a common trend for a common movement; residuals are autocorrelated and SEs too small.
  2. Differencing removes a stochastic trend and works on changes; detrending only removes a fitted deterministic trend, so for a random walk it leaves the unit root in place (detrending does not remove the unit root: median R² 0.091 and a 95th percentile of 0.491 over 500 draws). The choice depends on whether the series is difference-stationary or trend-stationary.
  3. R² measures in-sample fit only; residual autocorrelation, heteroskedasticity or omitted variables can make a high-R² model invalid.
