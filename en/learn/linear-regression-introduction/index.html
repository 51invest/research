Introduction to Linear Regression

Linear regression summarizes the average relationship between two variables with a line; slope, intercept and R-squared describe the sample, while significance and causal interpretation depend on design, assumptions and the limits of the evidence.

Research question

A hypothetical 48-month sample: a fund's monthly excess return y and the market's monthly excess return x. How much higher is y on average when x is 1 percentage point higher? Can the line predict and explain? This article is descriptive and inferential; it does not turn correlation into causation. All numbers are hypothetical, not real market data.

Data

Item Value
Sample size n 48
Mean of x 0.50% per month
Mean of y 0.80% per month
Sample standard deviation of x 2.00 percentage points
Sample standard deviation of y 2.80 percentage points
Correlation r 0.8571
Observed range of x about -3.6% to 4.2%

The data are hypothetical monthly observations, not seasonally adjusted; means are shown as % per month, while standard deviations and coefficient changes are shown in percentage points.

Method and assumptions

The least-squares line is:

y = a + b x

The slope is b = Σ(x-x̄)(y-ȳ) / Σ(x-x̄)² and the intercept is a = ȳ - b x̄ (OpenStax 12.3). The slope gives the average change in y per one-unit increase in x. R² = r² is the share of sample variance explained by the linear fit (OpenStax 12.3). Inference assumes linearity, independent errors, equal variance, approximately normal residuals and data from a well-designed random sample or randomized experiment; the correlation test has H0: ρ = 0 (OpenStax 12.4). These data are a hypothetical monthly time series, not a random sample, so the test results are only an approximate guide.

Results

b = 1.20, a = 0.20%, R² = 0.735. The regression standard error is 1.46 percentage points; SE(b) = 0.106, t = 11.29 (df = 46), p < 0.001, and the 95% interval is 0.99 to 1.41.

In the sample, a market excess return 1 percentage point higher is associated with a fund excess return 1.20 percentage points higher on average. At x = 1.00%, the predicted fund return is 0.20% + 1.20 × 1.00% = 1.40% per month. R² = 0.735 means about 73.5% of sample variance is explained; it is not a causal share and does not guarantee out-of-sample prediction.

Robustness

  • Robust SE(b) ≈ 0.11; the conclusion is unchanged.
  • Dropping the two largest absolute residuals gives b ≈ 1.16 and R² ≈ 0.71, with the sign unchanged.
  • Two halves give b ≈ 1.05 and 1.34, both significant, with overlapping intervals.
  • If x lies outside the observed range (for example 6.00%), the prediction is an extrapolation and the linear relationship may not hold (OpenStax 12.5).
  • Serial correlation or heteroskedasticity distorts conventional standard errors and t-values; use robust methods or a time-series model.

Limitations

Regression describes an average linear relationship within the sample. Omitted variables, measurement error, reverse causality and selection can bias b; a high R² does not show that the model is right. Forty-eight months cannot pin down a long-run relationship, and changes in market structure shift the parameters. Correlation is not causation (OpenStax 12.3). The numbers are hypothetical and are not investment advice; treat them as a methods exercise, not as an assessment of any real fund.

a = mean(y) - b * mean(x)
r2 = corr(x, y)^2
se_b = sqrt(sum(resid^2) / (n - 2) / sum((x - mean(x))^2))

Strength of conclusions

In this hypothetical sample, the relationship is strong in-sample (b = 1.20, 95% interval 0.99 to 1.41, R² = 0.735). It is an association and a prediction description, not a causal effect; extrapolation needs more evidence.

Knowledge-point review

control variables

Definition: Control variables are added to a regression to isolate the partial effect of a target variable.

Mechanism: Controls reduce omitted bias, but bad controls introduce overcontrol, collinearity and mediation; choice should follow causal graphs and mechanisms.

Example: For example, studying education's effect on income controls age, region and family background, but not post-graduation occupation, a mediator.

Boundary: More controls are not better; controlling mediators blocks the effect, controlling colliders creates bias, and measurement error and collinearity destabilise coefficients.

estimation diagnostics

Definition: Estimation diagnostics check whether regression results are credible through residuals, fit, collinearity, influence and specification.

Mechanism: Residual plots reveal nonlinearity and heteroskedasticity, VIF collinearity, Cook's distance influential points, and R-squared and standard errors fit and precision.

Example: For example, a funnel-shaped residual plot suggests heteroskedasticity, and a large Cook's distance flags influence.

Boundary: Diagnostics are not a formality; passing them does not prove causality, high R-squared can overfit, plots are subjective, and omitted-variable bias may not appear in residuals, so design matters.

heteroskedasticity

Definition: Heteroskedasticity means error variance changes across observations or regressors, violating homoskedasticity.

Mechanism: It biases conventional standard errors while coefficients may remain consistent; robust errors, weighted least squares or transformation address it.

Example: For example, consumption volatility rises with income, so error variance grows.

Boundary: Heteroskedasticity is not fatal; it mainly affects inference, robust errors help but are unstable in small samples, and omitted variables and nonlinearity can mimic it.

autocorrelation

Definition: Autocorrelation is correlation of errors over time, so adjacent or lagged errors are not independent.

Mechanism: It understates standard errors and inflates t-statistics; Newey-West, dynamic models or differencing help, but true dynamics and omitted variables must be distinguished.

Example: For example, monthly errors persist positively and ordinary significance is overstated.

Boundary: Autocorrelation is not only time series; spatial and grouped data correlate, robust errors cannot fix a wrong model, and lag choice and unit roots matter.

endogeneity

Definition: Endogeneity means a regressor correlates with the error, biasing coefficients through omitted variables, reverse causality, measurement error and simultaneity.

Mechanism: It cannot be solved by controlling everything; instrumental variables, experiments, panel fixed effects or structural models are needed, and direction and mechanism guide choice.

Example: For example, education affects income but ability affects both, so omitting ability biases the education coefficient upward.

Boundary: Endogeneity is not a technicality; it determines causal interpretation, instruments must be relevant and exogenous, fixed effects cannot fix time-varying omissions, and measurement error attenuates coefficients.

discrete and panel models

Definition: Discrete models handle binary, count or categorical outcomes, and panel models handle repeated observations of units over time.

Mechanism: Logit and probit suit binary, Poisson counts, and panel fixed or random effects control time-invariant individual differences.

Example: For example, default status uses logit, and multi-period firm investment uses panel fixed effects.

Boundary: Model choice is not automatic; discrete coefficients are nonlinear, fixed effects cannot estimate time-invariant variables, random effects assume no correlation with regressors, and clustered errors matter.

Exercises

  1. What is the unit of the slope 1.20?
  2. Does R² = 0.735 mean the model explains 73.5% of the causal effect?
  3. Why is a prediction at x = 6.00% uncertain?
Suggested answers
  1. Percentage points of y per percentage point of x: a 1 point higher x is associated with a 1.20 point higher y on average.
  2. No. R² is an in-sample variance share; it does not measure causation or guarantee out-of-sample prediction.
  3. At x = 6.00%, it is outside the observed range (about -3.6% to 4.2%), so it is an extrapolation and the linear relationship may fail.
