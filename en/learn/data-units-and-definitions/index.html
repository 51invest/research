Data Units and Statistical Definitions

Similar labels can hide different denominators, units, frequencies and seasonal adjustments; check definitions before comparing, and never fill missing values with zero.

ModuleRES-M04 · 数据口径
LevelFoundation
Read9 min · 12 sections

This article has an interactive demo ↓

Research question

Why can two numbers both called “unemployment” or “inflation” fail to be comparable? Data are not pure numbers detached from definitions. Units, denominators, frequency, seasonal adjustment, revisions, geography and price bases change meaning. This article provides a pre-comparison checklist and uses unemployment, price indices, GDP and debt to illustrate common errors.

Data and definitions

Units and denominators

  • Levels: persons, currency amounts, index points.
  • Rates: unemployment, interest, growth; the denominator must be stated.
  • Indices: set to 100 at a base period; the level has no direct economic unit.
  • Stocks: balances at a point in time, such as debt or assets.
  • Flows: amounts over a period, such as GDP, income or investment.

Suppose 8.5 million people are unemployed and the labor force is 170 million. The unemployment rate is 8.5 / 170 = 5.0%. That is a share of the labor force, not of the total population. Dividing 8.5 million by a population of 340 million gives about 2.5%, but that is a different indicator and should not be called the unemployment rate.

If a price index rises from 120 to 125, the increase is 125/120 - 1 ≈ 4.17%, not 5 percentage points and not 5%. The difference of 5 is an index-point change; only a growth-rate conversion gives a percentage change.

GDP is an annual or quarterly flow, while government debt is a stock at a point in time. “Debt rose by 2 trillion this year” (currency unstated in the quote; assume US dollars and an annual flow) must distinguish new borrowing during the year from the change in the outstanding balance. A debt-to-GDP ratio must state the period and definitions of both numerator and denominator.

The variable, population and sample conventions come from OpenStax: data are the values of variables, a population is the set of objects studied, a sample is a subset of the population, and variables are qualitative (categorical) or quantitative (numerical) [1].

Frequency, seasonal adjustment and revisions

  • Frequency: monthly, quarterly and annual values are not interchangeable; interpolating a quarterly value to monthly does not create new information.
  • Seasonal adjustment: adjusted series remove recurring calendar and seasonal variation to reveal trends [3]; unadjusted series retain seasonal movement. Do not mix them, and do not treat a seasonally adjusted monthly change as an annual rate.
  • Revisions: first releases may be revised. Record the data vintage, access time and release date. Using the latest revision in a historical backtest can introduce look-ahead bias.
  • Missing versus zero: missing means unknown and must not be filled with zero; zero can be a real observation. Missing values, discontinued series, non-disclosure and true zeros are different states.
  • Nominal versus real: nominal values are not adjusted for prices; real values use a price index or deflator. If nominal wages rise 5% and CPI rises 3%, real wages rise by about 1.05/1.03 - 1 ≈ 1.94%, not 2 percentage points.
  • Currency direction: USD/JPY means yen per dollar, while JPY/USD means dollars per yen; they are reciprocals. Record the quote direction, date and source.
  • Geography and institutions: borders, coverage, seasonal-adjustment methods and definitions change; cross-country comparisons cannot rely on labels alone.

What the ONS page states is that seasonal adjustment removes systematic calendar-related variation associated with the time of year so that consecutive periods can be compared [3] (verbatim in source 3's registration). The rule that adjusted and unadjusted series must not be mixed comes from this site's terminology register entry TERM-SEASONAL-ADJUSTMENT (seasonal adjustment; its disambiguation states the prohibition on mixing adjusted and unadjusted series). It is this site's own convention, not a sentence from the ONS page; the same applies to reading a seasonally adjusted monthly change as a year-on-year rate.

Examples

The four calculations used in this article (hypothetical figures, computed on the definitions stated here): the unemployment rate takes the labor force as denominator, 8.5 / 170 = 5.0%, while 8.5 / 340 = 2.5% using the population is a different indicator; index points are not percentages, so a CPI move from 120 to 125 is 125 / 120 - 1 = 4.17%, not 5 percentage points; a seasonally adjusted index moving from 1.03 to 1.05 is a 1.05 / 1.03 - 1 = 1.94% month-on-month change; and annualising a monthly 0.3% gives 0.3% x 12 = 3.6% on a simple basis but (1.003)^12 - 1 = 3.66% compounded - both are arithmetically correct, so the text must say which one it uses. Robustness and failure conditions

  • Splicing series after a definition change can turn a measurement change into an apparent economic change.
  • Comparing index levels without checking the base and weighting method can produce wrong growth rates.
  • Mixing first releases with later revisions can create look-ahead bias and non-reproducible results.
  • Ignoring seasonal, working-day and leap-year effects can turn calendar effects into trends.
  • Currency, unit and rounding errors amplify cross-country comparison errors.
  • Selection, survivorship and missing-data mechanisms can make descriptive statistics unrepresentative.

Reproduction checklist

  1. Record the source, series code, definition and denominator.
  2. State units, currency, price base and scale.
  3. Confirm frequency, observation period, release date and access time.
  4. Check seasonal adjustment, working-day adjustment and annualization.
  5. Distinguish nominal from real, stock from flow, and level from growth rate.
  6. Verify geography, institutional definitions and revision vintage.
  7. Mark missing values, zeros, discontinued series and estimates.
  8. Preserve raw data and transformations so others can reproduce the result.

Stating units explicitly is what makes numbers comparable: the International System of Units defines measurement through seven base units, as described by the NIST Physical Measurement Laboratory [2].

Strength of conclusions

Only after these checks can two numbers be described as comparable in a stated sense. Consistent definitions support descriptive comparison; they do not establish causation, predictive power or policy effects. If definitions differ, adjust them or state the limitation rather than treating the difference as an economic finding.

Knowledge-point review

units

Definition: Units are standards of measurement such as yuan, tonnes, kilowatt-hours, percentages and index points, defining meaning and comparability.

Mechanism: Conversion, dimensions and pricing basis affect calculations; mixed units cause order-of-magnitude errors, and real versus nominal and total versus per-capita must differ.

Example: For example, treating 100 million yuan as 10,000 yuan inflates a thousandfold, and treating a percentage as a decimal changes results.

Boundary: Units are not labels; hidden units, FX and index bases mislead, missing axis labels cause misreads, and dimensional checks belong before calculation.

currencies

Definition: A currency is the unit of denomination, and data in different currencies must be converted or FX-adjusted for comparison.

Mechanism: Exchange rates, timing and nominal versus real values matter; local currency, dollar and SDR bases differ and conversion adds volatility.

Example: For example, comparing GDP at market rates or PPP gives very different results.

Boundary: Currency is not simple conversion; FX timing, inflation and controls bias results, PPP does not suit all comparisons, and cross-border data need source and date.

stocks and flows

Definition: A stock is a quantity at a point in time, and a flow is a change over a period; flows accumulate into stocks, and stock change equals inflow minus outflow.

Mechanism: GDP and income are flows, while debt, assets and population are stocks; ratios such as debt/GDP mix stock and flow and need horizon and valuation care.

Example: For example, annual investment is a flow and the accumulated capital stock is a point value; a deficit is a flow and debt a stock.

Boundary: Stocks and flows must not be mixed; treating a flow as a stock misjudges scale, revaluation, FX and depreciation break simple accumulation, and ratios need numerator and denominator definitions.

data frequency

Definition: Frequency is the observation interval, such as daily, monthly, quarterly or annual, affecting volatility, sample size and comparability.

Mechanism: High-frequency data have more observations but noise and microstructure bias, low-frequency data are smooth but hide turns, and mixed frequencies need alignment or models.

Example: For example, daily return volatility exceeds annualised monthly volatility, and quarterly GDP is smoother than monthly indicators.

Boundary: Higher frequency is not always better; daily data have weekend and holiday gaps, low-frequency data are revised, and conversion adds interpolation and aggregation error, so direct comparison fails.

seasonal adjustment

Definition: Seasonal adjustment removes seasonal factors such as weather, holidays and production cycles to reveal trend and cycle.

Mechanism: Methods such as X-13ARIMA-SEATS estimate seasonal factors; adjusted data suit month-on-month analysis, while raw data suit year-on-year or specific work.

Example: For example, retail swings around Spring Festival, and adjusted data show the underlying trend better.

Boundary: Adjustment is not deleting reality; methods, samples and revisions change results, adjusted data can be misread as levels, and abnormal seasons and structural change break factors.

release dates and timing

Definition: Release date is when data first become public, determining when markets can see information, often with calendars and embargoes.

Mechanism: Data have preliminary, revised and final values, and release timing differs from the reference period; reactions depend on surprises and revisions.

Example: For example, GDP advance estimates come weeks after the quarter and are revised later, and traders watch timing, not just the number.

Boundary: Release date is not the data date; time zones, delays and leaks create information advantage, preliminary values are noisy, and using final values in backtests causes look-ahead bias.

data revisions

Definition: Revisions update published data with new information, method changes or rebasing.

Mechanism: Revisions affect history, growth rates and model parameters; preliminary and final values can differ greatly and revisions can be systematic.

Example: For example, an initial employment estimate later revised down sharply, changing the cyclical read.

Boundary: Revisions are not just error fixes; method changes and rebasing create breaks, real-time and final data differ, and backtests should use data available then, not the final vintage.

missing data and outliers

Definition: Missing data are absent observations, and outliers are values far from the distribution or mechanism, due to error, extreme events or structural change.

Mechanism: Missingness can be random, conditional or non-random; outliers may be errors or real extremes, handled by deletion, imputation, trimming or robust statistics.

Example: For example, survey nonresponse and sensor failures create missing data, while crisis returns are real tails, not errors.

Boundary: Missing and outliers must not be deleted blindly; deletion biases samples, imputation invents information, outliers can be the key signal, and handling needs mechanisms and sensitivity.

Exercises

  1. Index growth. A price index rises from 120 to 125. What is the growth rate, and why is “5%” wrong?
  2. Denominator. Unemployment is 8.5 million, the labor force is 170 million and the population is 340 million. What is the unemployment rate, and why not divide by population?
  3. Missing and zero. A monthly series is not published for one month. What happens if it is filled with zero, and how should it be recorded?
Suggested answers
  1. The growth rate is 125/120 - 1 ≈ 4.17%. The 5 is an index-point difference; index levels are not percentages and cannot be treated as a growth rate.
  2. The rate is 8.5 / 170 = 5.0%; the denominator is the labor force, not the total population. Dividing by population gives a different indicator, not the unemployment rate.
  3. Filling zero turns “unknown” into “nothing happened,” biasing means, growth rates and correlations. Keep the value missing, record why it was not published, and state the treatment in the analysis.
