Mathematical Foundations

Problem

Mathematical foundations address how to write an economic or financial problem as functions, constraints and computable steps, and when approximations hold or numerical solutions fail. It is a modelling language, not a pile of formulas; the same problem expressed as a ratio, a logarithm or a derivative yields different intuition and error.

Inputs

Inputs include variable definitions, units, time scale, data range and precision. State whether growth is year-on-year or period-on-period, prices are nominal or real, and returns are arithmetic or geometric before choosing a tool. Wrong inputs make later derivations meaningless.

Assumptions

Assumptions include functional form, continuity, differentiability, linearisation range, independence and numerical convergence. Common approximations: a first derivative for small changes; log differences for growth rates; normal approximations for large samples. Each has a valid range; outside it, error grows quickly[3].

Steps

Steps fall into four groups: write the problem (variables, units, relationship or objective, constraints); choose a representation (ratio, logarithm, derivative); solve (analytically or numerically, with convergence checks); and check boundaries (domain, error, stability and sensitivity)[4][5].

For a growth problem: end value = start value × (1 + g)^n; taking logs gives ln(end/start) = n × ln(1 + g). For small g, ln(1+g) ≈ g, so cumulative log growth over n years is about n×g. The approximation is decent at g=7% and n=10, but clearly understates at g=30%.

A worked example

Start with 100, grow at 7% a year for 10 years. The exact terminal value is 100×1.07^10≈196.72. Cumulative log growth is 10×ln(1.07)≈0.6766, and e^0.6766≈1.9672, almost the same. Replacing the log with 10×0.07=0.70 gives e^0.70≈2.0138, an overestimate of about 4.7 percentage points. The approximation worsens as growth rises:

Annual g ln(1+g) Linear approximation g Error g−ln(1+g) Error relative to ln(1+g)
5% 0.04879 0.05000 0.00121 2.5%
7% 0.06766 0.07000 0.00234 3.5%
10% 0.09531 0.10000 0.00469 4.9%
20% 0.18232 0.20000 0.01768 9.7%
30% 0.26236 0.30000 0.03764 14.3%

Small growth rates tolerate the linear approximation; at 30% it is visibly wrong. The numbers are teaching assumptions and can be checked with a calculator.

Interpreting results

Interpreting results must separate levels, growth rates and elasticities: levels depend on units, growth needs a base, elasticity is a unit-free ratio. Numerical solutions should report convergence criteria, iterations and error; optimisation results should state local versus global optimum and whether constraints bind[4][5].

Failure conditions

Failure conditions include discontinuity or non-differentiability on the domain; linearisation outside its range; singular or ill-conditioned matrices; local optima; non-convergent iterations; unit mismatch and dimensional error. Failure is not “miscalculation” but a mismatch between model assumptions and data conditions[4][5].

Reproduction

Reproduction requires saving code, data, parameters and random seeds; validate numerical results against analytical solutions, known special cases or Monte Carlo. Sensitivity analysis should vary key parameters and initial values and report a range, not a single number[1][2][3][4][5].

Knowledge-point review

functions

Definition: A function maps an input to an output, with a domain of allowed inputs and a range of possible outputs.

Mechanism: Linear functions change by a fixed slope, exponential functions by a fixed ratio, and nonlinear functions capture saturation, thresholds and interactions; finance uses them for prices, returns and models.

Example: For example, simple interest grows linearly, compound interest exponentially, and demand curves can be nonlinear.

Boundary: A function is not causal proof; fitted relations can be spurious, extrapolation outside the domain is risky, and breaks and jumps defeat one function, so mechanisms and assumptions matter.

ratios and proportions

Definition: A ratio compares two quantities, a percentage multiplies by 100, and ratios standardise quantities of different scale.

Mechanism: Ratios remove scale effects, but denominator choice, units and base period define meaning; per-capita, debt ratio and margin are ratios.

Example: For example, 5 percent GDP growth is relative to last year, and debt/GDP is a stock-to-flow ratio.

Boundary: A ratio is not an absolute quantity; small bases exaggerate percentages, zero or negative denominators are meaningless, and ratios can hide distribution and composition.

logarithms

Definition: A logarithm transforms multiplicative relations into additive ones, commonly natural or base-10.

Mechanism: Logs compress large values, stabilise variance and suit growth and heavy tails; log differences approximate percentage changes, and log axes show equal ratios as equal distances.

Example: For example, 1 to 10 and 10 to 100 are equally spaced on a log axis, both tenfold.

Boundary: Logs are not universal; zero and negatives are undefined, approximations hold for small changes, and transformation changes interpretation and errors, so back-transformation needs care.

growth

Definition: Growth is an increase over time, described by period-on-period, year-on-year, CAGR and exponential growth.

Mechanism: Simple growth uses a base, CAGR annualises multi-period growth, and exponential growth compounds at a fixed ratio; doubling time is about 70 divided by the percentage rate.

Example: For example, 100 to 121 over two years is 10 percent CAGR, and 7 percent annual growth doubles in about a decade.

Boundary: Growth is not one number; base, inflation, population and FX matter, negative growth and volatility distort CAGR, and extrapolating exponentials ignores constraints.

calculus

Definition: Calculus studies rates of change and accumulation: derivatives are instantaneous rates and integrals continuous totals.

Mechanism: Derivatives support marginal analysis, optimisation and sensitivity; integrals turn flows into stocks and densities into totals, used for duration, probability and continuous compounding.

Example: For example, marginal cost is the derivative of total cost with respect to output, and continuous compounding is exponential accumulation.

Boundary: Calculus is not only for smooth functions; jumps, heavy tails and discrete data need other tools, local derivatives do not establish global or causal claims, and assumptions matter.

linear algebra

Definition: Linear algebra studies vectors, matrices, linear transformations and systems, underpinning regression, portfolio optimisation and machine learning.

Mechanism: Matrix multiplication represents linear combinations, eigen- and singular-value decompositions reduce dimensions, and least squares solves regression coefficients.

Example: For example, an asset return matrix times a weight vector gives portfolio return, and a covariance matrix describes risk.

Boundary: Linear algebra is not the model; linear assumptions can fail, singular matrices and collinearity make solutions unstable, and eigenvalue interpretation depends on scaling and domain knowledge.

optimisation and numerical methods

Definition: Optimisation finds a maximum or minimum of an objective under constraints, and numerical methods approximate solutions iteratively.

Mechanism: Gradient descent steps along the negative gradient, Newton uses second-order information, constrained methods use Lagrange or projection; convergence, local optima and stability matter.

Example: For example, portfolio optimisation maximises expected return under risk budgets using gradients or quadratic programming.

Boundary: Optimisation is not finding the true optimum; non-convexity, noise and conflicting constraints yield local solutions, overfitting and numerical error mislead, and a wrong objective optimises the wrong thing.

Data and institutional check: mathematical foundations

Before using "mathematical foundations" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns logarithm.

Third, the institutional boundary. calculus is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: mathematical foundations

Learning "mathematical foundations" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "logarithm": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "calculus" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of mathematical foundations still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind logarithm reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "mathematical foundations" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "logarithm" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Concept check: explain why growth uses a log difference while level changes cannot simply be subtracted.
  2. Calculation: using ln(1.07)≈0.0677, estimate the 10-year cumulative multiple at 7% and compare with 1.07^10.
  3. Conditions: give one case where linearisation fails.
Reference answers
  1. Growth is multiplicative. A log difference log(y_t/y_{t−1}) turns “+10% then +10%” into the additive 0.0953+0.0953, whereas a level difference depends on units and base and cannot be added across series.
  2. 10×ln(1.07)≈0.6766, e^0.6766≈1.967, cumulative growth about 96.7%; 1.07^10≈1.967, the same. Using 0.70 gives e^0.70≈2.014, an overestimate of about 4.7 percentage points.
  3. 30% annual growth is the counterexample: ln(1.3)≈0.262 but the linear approximation uses 0.30; after ten years e^3≈20.09 versus the true 1.3^10≈13.79. When growth is large or curvature strong, use the exact form or a higher-order expansion.

  2. Mechanism chain. Write the transmission from "mathematical foundations" to "logarithm" in three steps and name the step most dependent on an unstated assumption.
