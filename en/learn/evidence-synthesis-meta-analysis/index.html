Evidence Synthesis and Meta-Analysis

A meta-analysis is not an average of paper conclusions; it puts the question, effect sizes, samples, bias and heterogeneity on one table to judge whether the pooled effect is credible.

Problem and scope

A single paper is often amplified into "research proves." But one study has a small sample, different definitions and selection bias; its result may be noise. Evidence synthesis systematically searches, screens, codes, weights and tests heterogeneity to produce a conclusion with uncertainty and scope. Meta-analysis is one quantitative pooling method within evidence synthesis, not the whole of it.

This article follows seven steps: PICO question, search and screening, effect size, weighting model, heterogeneity, publication bias and applicability. The aim is not software operation but reading a forest plot: why some studies receive more weight, and why a "significant pooled effect" can still be untrustworthy. All numbers are teaching assumptions, not a reproduction of any real literature.

Start with PICO

A question that cannot be written as PICO cannot be synthesised well. P is the population, I the intervention or exposure, C the comparator, O the outcome. For example, "the effect of a vocational programme on employment for low-income youth": P is low-income youth aged 18–24, I is an eight-week programme, C is no training or placebo, O is formal employment after 12 months. If the question is "does training work," there are a hundred outcomes and no boundary for search or pooling.

After PICO, write inclusion and exclusion criteria: design (randomised, quasi-experimental, observational), time window, geography, language, publication type and outcome measurement. The criteria must be written before the search, not changed after seeing results. Otherwise the arrow is drawn before the target.

Search, screening and coding

Search at least three source types: databases (Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, CNKI and others), grey literature (working papers, government reports, dissertations) and reference chasing. Searching only English journals misses local policy evaluations; searching only published papers overstates effects because negative results are harder to publish.

Screening usually has two stages: title/abstract, then full text. Record the number and reason for exclusion at each stage and draw a PRISMA flow diagram. Two independent reviewers should screen and calculate agreement, with a third adjudicating disagreements; a single reviewer should at least do a sampled recheck.

Design the coding sheet in advance: sample size, population, intervention intensity, comparator, outcome definition, follow-up, effect size, standard error, adjusted variables and study quality. Coding is not copying the abstract; it translates each study into comparable numbers. Coding errors are more common than model errors and harder to fix.

Effect sizes and weights

Studies report different outcomes: mean difference, standardised mean difference (SMD), risk ratio (RR) or odds ratio (OR). Convert to one scale before pooling. Continuous outcomes often use Hedges' g; binary outcomes use RR or OR. State the conversion formula; do not treat OR as RR.

Weights depend on precision. Fixed-effect models weight by 1/variance: larger samples and smaller standard errors get more weight. Random-effects models add a between-study variance τ² to the weights, balancing large and small studies. Fixed effects ask whether the studies share one true effect; random effects ask for the mean of a distribution of effects. Different questions, different models; do not choose by which result is more significant.

Heterogeneity and publication bias

Heterogeneity is central, not an appendix. The Q test asks whether observed differences exceed sampling error; I² is the share of total variation explained by between-study differences. I² = 0 is consistent, 50% moderate, above 75% high. With high heterogeneity, a pooled average may be meaningless; use subgroup analysis or narrative synthesis.

Publication bias is another trap. Significant results are easier to publish, so pooled effects are biased upward. A funnel plot graphs effect size against precision; small, large-effect studies should scatter at the bottom. Asymmetry suggests bias, but the plot is unreliable with high heterogeneity and few studies; Egger's test is a clue, not a verdict.

A numerical example

Take three teaching studies with effects 0.20, 0.35 and 0.50 and standard errors 0.10, 0.12 and 0.15. Fixed-effect weights are 1/SE²: 100, 69.44 and 44.44, total 213.88. The weighted mean is (100×0.20 + 69.44×0.35 + 44.44×0.50)/213.88 = (20 + 24.30 + 22.22)/213.88 = 0.311.

Q is the sum of squared deviations from the weighted mean times the weights: 100×(0.20−0.311)² = 1.23; 69.44×(0.35−0.311)² = 0.104; 44.44×(0.50−0.311)² = 1.59; total 2.92. With df = 3−1 = 2, Q = 2.92 is below the critical value of about 5.99, so the homogeneity hypothesis is not rejected; I² = max(0,(2.92−2)/2.92) = 31.5%, low to moderate. The example shows that effects from 0.20 to 0.50 can look far apart while sampling error explains part of the spread.

If the third standard error falls to 0.05, its weight rises to 400, Q and I² increase sharply. A more precise study with a different conclusion makes heterogeneity more, not less, important. Meta-analysis is not "the largest sample wins"; it is "precision gets weight, while between-study differences are tested."

Reading a forest plot

A forest plot is a visual audit of the pooling, not decoration. Each row is a study: a square marks the point estimate, its size usually reflects the weight, and the horizontal line is the confidence interval. The vertical line at zero (or one, for ratios) is the null. The diamond at the bottom is the pooled effect and its confidence interval. If the diamond crosses the null, the pooled effect is not statistically distinguishable from no effect at the chosen level; if it does not, the average is significant, but heterogeneity and bias still decide whether it is credible.

The plot reveals four things a table can hide. First, whether a large study dominates: one huge square with narrow whiskers can pull the diamond toward its own result. Second, whether studies disagree in direction: intervals that straddle the null on both sides suggest a real subgroup split, not just noise. Third, whether the pooled confidence interval is implausibly narrow: pooling many small studies can produce a precise average from imprecise evidence, a warning sign of overfitting the model. Fourth, whether the scale is consistent: an OR and an RR plotted on the same axis is a coding error, not a finding.

A practical workflow follows the plot. Define the question and outcomes; pre-register the protocol; search and de-duplicate; screen in two stages; code study-level data; convert effect sizes; choose fixed or random effects based on the question; calculate Q, I² and τ²; inspect subgroups; test publication bias; rate the evidence; write the conclusion with boundaries. Each step leaves a record, so a reader can reproduce the decision rather than trust the diamond.

The workflow also needs a stopping rule. If the search finds no comparable studies, the correct output is a narrative synthesis, not a pooled estimate. If the studies are comparable but of low quality, the correct output is a cautious conclusion with a quality caveat. A meta-analysis that forces a pool because the software offers one is a method failure.

Software defaults are not methodological choices. A package may default to a random-effects model, a particular τ² estimator, a continuity correction or a specific weighting scheme. Each default changes the result. The analyst must state which estimator was used, why it fits the question, and how the result changes under a plausible alternative. A sensitivity table with three models is more informative than one precise-looking forest plot.

Evidence grading is not a score attached to the forest plot. GRADE starts high for randomised evidence and lowers it for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias; it can raise observational evidence for large effects or dose-response. The final label—high, moderate, low or very low—is a judgment about confidence in the effect estimate, not about whether the effect is large. A meta-analysis can produce a precise average and still be low-confidence evidence. The protocol should name who will make that judgment and which disagreements require a third reviewer. The protocol is therefore part of the result, not paperwork.

Data conventions

Convention one: harmonise effect direction and scale. SMD, RR and OR are not interchangeable; OR cannot be read as RR, especially when event rates exceed 20%. Coding must state comparator, time window and outcome definition.

Convention two: report weights, model and heterogeneity together. Fixed effects weight by 1/SE²; SE 0.10/0.12/0.15 gives weights 100/69.4/44.4. With Q=2.92 and df=2, I² is about 31.5%, and a random-effects model changes the result.

Convention three: publication bias needs search scope, grey literature and power. With fewer than ten studies, funnel plots and Egger's test cannot diagnose; unpublished nulls and unregistered outcomes still inflate the pooled effect.

Convention four: leave a reproducibility package. Search strings, screening table, coding sheet, effect-size conversion, weights, model and sensitivity analyses must be recomputable; a forest plot alone cannot show whether 0.311 is a data result or an analyst choice.

Boundaries and counterexamples

First, do not pool different questions. Combining "training and employment" with "training and wages" produces a meaningless average. PICO must be unified first.

Second, do not look only at significance. An effect of 0.05 can be significant in a large sample but policy-relevant almost not at all; an effect of 0.30 with a large standard error may be too uncertain. Report effect size, confidence interval and clinical/policy meaning.

Third, do not ignore study quality. Randomised versus observational, pre-registered versus post hoc, peer-reviewed versus working paper have different evidence levels. Quality ratings such as GRADE belong in the conclusion, not an appendix.

Fourth, do not treat I² as an absolute threshold. I² depends on sample size and number of studies and is unstable with few studies. Look at direction, source and subgroups, not one percentage.

Fifth, do not use a funnel plot alone. With fewer than ten studies, the test has little power; Egger's test is also affected by heterogeneity. Pre-registration, grey-literature search, trim-and-fill sensitivity analysis and disclosure of unresolved bias are more robust.

Sixth, meta-analysis is not a permanent answer. New studies, data and methods change the result; state the search date and update plan. Treating one meta-analysis as final is as dangerous as treating one paper as truth.

Additional boundary one: meta-analysis is not averaging conclusions. It defines the question, searches, screens and codes before weighting by standard error. Three studies with SE 0.10/0.12/0.15 get fixed weights 100/69.4/44.4; effects 0.20/0.35/0.50 give a weighted mean of about 0.311, and another model changes it.

Additional boundary two: heterogeneity is not noise. With Q=2.92 and df=2, I² is about 31.5%, low to moderate; with Q=12 and df=2 it is about 83%, where a pooled mean hides differences in population, dose and outcome.

Additional boundary three: publication bias cannot be diagnosed from a funnel plot. With fewer than ten studies, funnel plots and Egger's test lack power; unpublished nulls, unregistered outcomes and undisclosed analysis paths all inflate effects.

Additional boundary four: GRADE rates confidence, not effect size. Risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias can downgrade evidence; observational studies start lower. A meta-analysis can compute 0.311 but cannot decide whether the effect is worth acting on.

One-page checklist

  • Write PICO, inclusion/exclusion criteria and outcomes before searching.

  • Search databases, grey literature and references; record exclusions and counts.

  • Report screening with a PRISMA flow diagram; double-screen or recheck a sample.

  • Convert to one effect size and state the formula; choose fixed or random effects by question.

  • Report Q, I², τ² and subgroup analysis, not only a pooled effect.

  • Check publication bias with funnel plot, Egger, trim-and-fill and grey literature.

  • Rate evidence with GRADE or similar; state the population and boundaries.

  • Label every teaching number as an assumption; never pass it off as a real literature result.

  • Put search strings, screening table, coding sheet and sensitivity analyses in a reproducibility package so a third party can recompute 0.311.

  • State the conclusion for a population, dose and outcome; do not write 'the research proves it works'.

  • For syntheses with fewer than ten studies, state that power is insufficient; do not treat a symmetric funnel plot as proof of no bias.

Common misreads

Misread one: more studies always make a better meta-analysis. Pooling different questions, doses and outcomes makes bias more precise; check PICO and inclusion criteria before counting studies.

Misread two: high I² forbids pooling. High heterogeneity weakens the interpretation of an average, not synthesis itself; use subgroups, meta-regression or narrative synthesis, but state why.

Misread three: a symmetric funnel plot means no publication bias. Funnel plots and Egger's test lack power with fewer than ten studies; unpublished nulls and unregistered outcomes can still inflate effects.

Misread four: a pooled effect can become policy directly. 0.311 is a weighted average, not a causal effect for any population; generalisation depends on population, implementation and dose, and GRADE rates confidence.

Misread five: more studies mean stronger evidence. Ten poorly designed, overlapping studies are worth less than three preregistered studies with consistent definitions; information depends on bias risk, independence and outcome consistency, not the number of PDFs.,Misread six: low GRADE means a small effect. GRADE rates confidence in the effect estimate, not the effect size; a large effect from a high-bias study can still be low-confidence evidence.

Exercises

  1. Write 'does vocational training raise employment' as PICO and specify one outcome.
  2. Write at least four inclusion/exclusion criteria and explain why they must precede the search.
  3. List databases, grey literature and reference chasing with one example each.
  4. Explain when to use SMD, RR and OR, and why OR cannot be treated as RR.
  5. Three studies have SE 0.10/0.12/0.15; compute fixed-effect weights 1/SE².
  6. Using effects 0.20/0.35/0.50 and the weights above, compute the weighted mean.
  7. Compute I² for Q=2.92 and df=2, and classify it as low, moderate or high.
  8. With high heterogeneity, why can a pooled average be meaningless? Give two alternatives.
  9. What are the limits of a funnel plot and Egger's test, especially with fewer than ten studies?
  10. List the five GRADE downgrade domains and state whether GRADE rates confidence or effect size.
  11. Software defaults for fixed or random effects change conclusions; design a three-model sensitivity table.
  12. Explain in one paragraph that meta-analysis is a reproducible evidence audit, not an average of conclusions.
