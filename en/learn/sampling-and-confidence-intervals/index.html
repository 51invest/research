Sampling and Confidence Intervals

A confidence interval combines a sample mean with sampling error to give a range for the population mean; 95% means about 95% of intervals from repeated samples would cover the mean, not that this interval has a 95% probability of containing it.

Research question

A hypothetical strategy has 36 monthly returns, a mean of 1.20% and a standard deviation of 2.40%. Can 1.20% be used to infer the population mean, and how variable is another sample? This article builds a confidence interval, explains standard errors, the t distribution and coverage, and notes that intervals cannot repair bias. All numbers are hypothetical.

Data

Item Value Unit
Sample size n 36 Months
Sample mean x̄ 1.20 % per month
Sample SD s 2.40 Percentage points
Standard error s/√n 0.40 Percentage points
95% confidence interval 0.39 to 2.01 % per month

This is a hypothetical simple random sample; raw observations, sampling frame and missing-data rules are not shown.

Method and assumptions

The sample mean is centred on the population mean with standard error σ/√n; with independent observations and a large enough n, it is approximately normal (OpenStax 7.1). When σ is unknown, use s and a t distribution with n-1 degrees of freedom:

SE = s / √n
CI = x̄ ± t(α/2, n-1) × SE

The 95% interval uses t(0.975, 35) = 2.030 (OpenStax 8.2; NIST notes that width depends on the standard deviation and sample size).

Assumptions: simple random sampling, independent observations, approximate normality, and an s that represents population variability. Bias shifts the centre; dependence understates uncertainty.

Results

SE = 2.40/√36 = 0.40 points.

Margin = 2.030×0.40 = 0.812.

95% interval = 1.20 ± 0.812, or 0.39% to 2.01%.

Repeated samples would produce intervals that cover the population mean about 95% of the time; a single interval either covers it or not, so [0.39, 2.01] is not a 95% probability statement about the mean. The 1.62-point width comes mainly from monthly volatility. With n = 100, SE falls to 0.24 and the interval becomes 0.72% to 1.68% (same mean and s); at n = 36, a 99% interval is wider, 0.11% to 2.29%.

Diagram: center, width, coverage

Robustness

Using z = 1.96 gives 0.42% to 1.98%, close to the t result; the gap grows in small samples. Raising the confidence level or cutting the sample widens the interval. If dropping the best month lowers the mean sharply, the result depends on few observations. Bootstrap resampling can check non-normal intervals but not selection bias. Serial correlation, overlapping periods or fund clustering make conventional errors too small; use robust or clustered errors. Failure conditions: convenience samples, survivorship bias, non-random missingness and small, heavy-tailed samples.

Limitations

The interval covers sampling error, not bias. Coverage depends on the design and model; financial samples are rarely simple random samples, and fund histories carry survivorship and backfill bias. The interval says nothing about economic meaning or causation. Extreme values and small, non-normal samples can distort it. Numbers are hypothetical.

Reproduction

  1. Fix the sampling frame, period, frequency and missing rules.
  2. Compute n, x̄ and s.
  3. Compute SE = s/√n.
  4. Choose the confidence level and t quantile with n-1 df.
  5. Compute the interval; report units, period and assumptions.
  6. Check outliers, sub-periods and dependence.
se = s / sqrt(n)
margin = qt(0.975, df=n-1) * se
ci = (mean - margin, mean + margin)

Strength of conclusions

For the hypothetical sample, the 95% interval for mean monthly return is 0.39% to 2.01%. This describes sampling uncertainty, not a forecast or proof of skill. Bias or dependence would make it too narrow.

Knowledge-point review

confidence intervals

Definition: A confidence interval surrounds an estimate and covers the true parameter at a stated rate over repeated samples.

Mechanism: It uses the estimate, standard error and critical value; a 95 percent interval does not mean the parameter has a 95 percent probability of lying inside.

Example: For example, a mean of 10 with standard error 2 gives an approximate 95 percent interval of 6.1 to 13.9.

Boundary: A confidence interval is not a probability statement; the frequentist meaning is repeated coverage, model error, bias and multiple comparisons reduce coverage, and a narrow interval is not importance.

hypothesis testing

Definition: Hypothesis testing sets a null and alternative and uses sample data to decide whether to reject the null.

Mechanism: Test statistics, significance levels and p-values measure evidence; a p-value is the probability of a result at least as extreme under the null, not the probability the null is true.

Example: For example, comparing two means, a p-value below 0.05 is called significant but does not imply a large or causal effect.

Boundary: Testing is not proof; significance depends on sample size, multiple comparisons and specification, p-values omit effect size and practical importance, and rejecting the null does not prove the alternative.

effect size

Definition: Effect size is the practical magnitude of a result, such as a mean difference, standardised difference, correlation or risk difference, independent of sample size.

Mechanism: It helps judge practical importance; standardised effects aid cross-study comparison but need units and context.

Example: For example, a drug lowers blood pressure by 5 mmHg, statistically significant but clinically limited.

Boundary: Effect size is not a p-value; large samples make tiny effects significant, standardisation hides units and distributions, and effect estimates still suffer measurement and design bias.

statistical power

Definition: Statistical power is the probability of correctly rejecting the null when the alternative is true, usually one minus the type II error rate.

Mechanism: Power rises with sample size, effect size, significance level and falls with variance; low power misses real effects, while very high power makes tiny differences significant.

Example: For example, a small sample detecting a small effect may have 30 percent power and often miss it.

Boundary: Power is not a post hoc excuse; low-power studies are unreliable, post-hoc power is contested, high power does not remove design bias, and measurement and multiple testing still matter.

Bayesian updating and multiple testing

Definition: Bayesian updating combines a prior and likelihood into a posterior, and multiple testing controls error rates when testing many hypotheses at once.

Mechanism: The posterior is proportional to prior times likelihood and updates with data; Bonferroni and FDR methods control false positives.

Example: For example, a prior that a strategy is unlikely to work keeps the posterior conservative after limited evidence, and testing 100 factors needs a corrected threshold.

Boundary: Bayes is not arbitrary subjectivity; priors affect results and posteriors depend on models; correction reduces power, and uncorrected significance is often data snooping.

Exercises

  1. Explain the correct and a common incorrect reading of a 95% confidence interval.
  2. With n = 100, mean 1.20% and s = 2.40%, find the 95% interval (t = 1.984).
  3. What happens if only surviving funds are surveyed?
Suggested answers
  1. Correct: about 95% of intervals from repeated sampling cover the mean. Incorrect: this interval has a 95% probability of containing the mean.
  2. SE = 0.24; margin = 1.984×0.24 ≈ 0.476; the interval is 0.72% to 1.68%.
  3. The centre is too high because closed funds are excluded; the interval cannot repair survivorship bias.
