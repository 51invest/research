Alternative Data and Complex Systems: Signal, Noise and Networks

Alternative data is not “more data” but data from a different generating process: satellite images, card transactions, job postings, shipping and social text. Complex systems remind us that variables have feedback, thresholds and network contagion, so linear extrapolation often fails.

Research question

Can alternative data provide signals that traditional data miss? The answer depends on three questions: whom the data cover, what behaviour generates them, and how much noise they contain. Complex systems add that signals can be amplified or lost through thresholds, feedback and networks, so linear extrapolation fails[1].

Data

Satellite images, card transactions, job postings, ship tracking, social text, sensors and so on. Record coverage, time span, sampling frequency, missing mechanism and licence terms. Coverage bias can be more dangerous than noise: seeing only big cities or active users biases the conclusion[2].

Method

Signal-to-noise can be written:

SNR=SignalNoiseSNR = \frac{Signal}{Noise}SNR=NoiseSignal​

If signal mean is 60 and noise standard deviation 40, SNR is 1.5. Complex-system analysis also examines degree distribution, clustering, thresholds and feedback loops; these structures make the same shock produce different outcomes at different nodes[3].

Results

Suppose a job-postings dataset predicts industry employment two weeks ahead but covers only cities where listed companies are headquartered. In-sample correlation is 0.6; extended nationwide it falls to 0.2, showing coverage bias. In another network model, a few highly connected nodes failing triggers cascading defaults; a linear model underestimates tail risk.

Robustness

Change coverage, time windows, frequency and network construction; check whether conclusions hold. Alternative data carry “data mining” risk: many signals tried before one significant result, requiring multiple-testing correction and out-of-sample validation[4].

Limitations

First, coverage bias; second, changed generating mechanism; third, licence and privacy limits; fourth, noise and missingness; fifth, unstable network structure; sixth, overfitting. Under these conditions, alternative data may be “old noise in a new bottle”.

Reproduction

Record source, coverage, cleaning and feature construction; report in-sample and out-of-sample results; check multiple comparisons; disclose network and threshold assumptions. Reproduction is not repeating a correlation but letting readers judge the conditions under which the signal holds.

Reading and review

The first question about alternative data is not how big it is, but who is covered and who is missing. Using food-delivery data from tier-one cities to infer national consumption treats urban white-collar rhythms as the national rhythm; using app downloads to forecast revenue mixes channel stuffing with organic demand. Run a coverage audit first: source, time span, missingness and selection. Then a signal-to-noise audit: can a change be separated from noise, and is the threshold stable? Finally a network audit: can one node's anomaly amplify through supply chains or social ties? Only when all three pass is a forecast worth discussing; one pass buys a hypothesis at most.

Reading checklist

Alternative data also needs an interpretability check: if a variable correlates with the target without a mechanism, it may fail in another city or period. Draw the data-generating process and mark who records it, why, and where it is missing. When coverage and mechanism are unclear, the safest conclusion is that the data cannot support causal inference.

Further check

Before applying “Alternative Data and Complex Systems: Signal, Noise and Networks”, test the weakest link between “Coverage and generating mechanism” and “Signal-to-noise and nonlinearity”. Write down the observable proxy, the direction of the causal link and the condition under which the link reverses. Then ask whether the data are measured at the same frequency and definition as the decision, and whether a cost or lag sits between the signal and the outcome. If the chain cannot be stated without hand-waving, the result is a description, not a usable claim. Run one counterexample and one stress case before extending the conclusion to another market, period or population.

One last check

Finally, record the data’s collection boundary and refresh lag. A signal that arrives after the decision window has already closed is a historical description, not a forecast.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Alternative Data and Complex Systems: Signal, Noise and Networks"

The subject of this article is "Alternative Data and Complex Systems: Signals, Noise and Networks". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Coverage and generating mechanism". Column two states the transmission channel of "Signal-to-noise and nonlinearity". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Networks, thresholds and contagion". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Signal-to-noise and nonlinearity" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Networks, thresholds and contagion is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Review sentence

Write one sentence only: "Under the ____ convention, a change in ____ affects ____ through ____; if ____ appears, the claim fails." The four blanks are the unit, the variable, the channel and the falsifier. If the sentence cannot be written, at least one of the four columns is still empty; if it can be written but cannot be observed, the falsifier is wrong. On every data update, reread this sentence before reading the charts.

Boundary case: A signal with SNR 1.5 after three network layers: how much remains?

A signal has a mean of 60 and noise standard deviation of 40, for a signal-to-noise ratio of 1.5. If cleaning cuts noise to 30, SNR rises to 2.0. If network propagation amplifies the shock 1.5 times while adding 20 of noise, SNR becomes 90 ÷ 50 = 1.8. If one of three nodes fails, the clustering coefficient falls from 0.6 to 0.4, the shock no longer cascades, but the local signal is also weaker.

The conclusion reverses when feedback and thresholds break linear extrapolation: near a threshold, a small shock can be amplified; in a sparse network, the same shock stays local. The boundary is that alternative data come from a different generating process, not simply “more data”; complex systems require network, threshold and feedback analysis, not only linear regression.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not a data product.
