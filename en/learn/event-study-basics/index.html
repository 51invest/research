Event-Study Basics

After removing normal returns with a market model, 40 hypothetical firms show a three-day average cumulative abnormal return of 1.9% with a t value of 4.8; a control window with no injected effect still reads 0.1% in this sample, which is the sampling noise such estimates carry before clustering and model choice are considered.

Research question

Forty hypothetical firms make an announcement on the same event day. Does the event produce abnormal returns? A market model supplies normal returns, the three-day cumulative abnormal return CAR[-1,+1] measures the reaction, and a second three-day window that carries no injected effect by construction (t = −60 to −58) serves as a control. The return series is generated by an in-repo script over 131 trading days (t = −125 to +5) and is not real data.

Method and assumptions

The market model r_i,t = α_i + β_i r_m,t + ε_i,t is fitted firm by firm with least squares on the 100 trading days of the estimation window; abnormal returns AR are the event-window actual returns minus those fitted predictions, and CAR accumulates them across the window. The normal returns are predictions from a fitted line, and because data do not fall exactly on that line, the predictions are only approximate (OpenStax, 12.3). The estimation window ends at t = -21, leaving t = -20 to -2 as a gap that lowers the chance of anticipation leakage entering the parameter estimates. Both windows should be chosen by a pre-specified rule before the event outcome is seen; adjusting them afterwards amounts to repeated trials and inflates significance. Assumptions: the estimation window is uncontaminated by the event, residuals are independent and identically distributed, and event days are not clustered. The MacKinlay (1997) abstract states the purpose verbatim as "The goal of an event study is to measure the effects of an economic event on the value of firms." and the premise as "Event study methods exploit the fact that, given rationality in the marketplace, the effects of an event will be reflected immediately in security prices."; the same abstract says the paper describes "event study methods ... including some of the potential complications" [1]. Only the abstract on the RePEc/IDEAS index record was retrieved here, and that record notes that full-text access is restricted to AEA members, so these three attributions stop at the abstract and are not extended to the article's own argument.

Results

Mean CAR is 1.9% with a cross-sectional SD of 2.5% and t = 4.8; 25% of firms (10 of 40) have a negative CAR. All 40 firms share one event day, so cross-sectional independence does not hold, and this t value is only an upper bound for the reason given under Robustness. The injected effect is 1.5%, so the estimate sits about 0.4 percentage points above it, roughly 1.0 realized standard error (0.40 percentage points) - within sampling error. The theoretical three-day CAR SD is sqrt(3) × 1.2% = 2.1%, while the realized SD is 2.5%, because each firm's α and β are estimated from 100 days and that estimation error also enters the CAR. The control window (t = −60 to −58) reads 0.1% with t = 0.5 in this sample: it is indistinguishable from zero but not exactly zero, which is the sampling noise of 40 firms rather than an event effect.

Robustness

  • A market-adjusted model, or added size and value factors, would change the point estimate; report both the baseline and an alternative benchmark.
  • When events cluster on one day, the cross-sectional t overstates significance and the remedy is to aggregate by day and use clustered standard errors; here all 40 firms share a single event day, so cross-sectional independence fails by construction and that day offers no degrees of freedom for the aggregation.
  • The control window sits inside the estimation window, so its "no injection" is a property of the construction: it demonstrates only that the test does not manufacture significance out of nothing, and it is not an independent empirical placebo. A placebo of the latter kind needs a span that enters neither the fit nor the event count.
  • Judging whether the market model is adequate takes residual analysis, since a single fit statistic is not validation (NIST/SEMATECH, 4.4.4); a wrong functional form, heteroskedasticity and correlated residuals all change the standard errors.
  • Inspect the CAR distribution and outliers so the mean is not driven by a few firms, and check whether information leaked before the event.

Limitations

The simulation has one clean event day; real events involve uncertain dates, leakage, contemporaneous news and trading costs. The market model may omit risk factors, and with only 40 firms the t value relies on approximate normality. The result describes an average abnormal return; it does not explain a mechanism or establish causation.

Reproduction

  1. Generate 131 trading days of market returns and 40 firm return series with the fixed seed.
  2. Fit α and β firm by firm on the 100-day estimation window with least squares.
  3. Compute AR and CAR over the event window t = −1 to +1.
  4. Compute the cross-sectional mean, SD, standard error and t value.
  5. Repeat steps 3 and 4 over t = −60 to −58 as the control.

Every figure in this article comes from one run of scripts/simulate-event-study.mjs: with no arguments it prints the parameters and the results, --selftest asserts the structure and a 200-replication calibration, and --check compares the table in this article against the run.

Strength of conclusions

In the simulation the event window carries a significantly positive abnormal return, while the control window reads 0.1% (t = 0.5) in this draw - indistinguishable from zero but not exactly zero. That supports the claim that the procedure does not manufacture significance, not the claim that a window without an event must equal zero. One event, 40 firms and a three-day window cannot identify how real announcements are priced, and the average CAR is not a causal mechanism.

Knowledge-point review

returns

Definition: Return is the rate of change in price or value: simple return is end minus start over start, log return is the difference in logs.

Mechanism: Simple returns aggregate across assets, log returns add over time; dividends, splits and fees must be included, and frequency affects annualisation.

Example: For example, 100 to 110 is a 10 percent simple return and about 9.53 percent log return.

Boundary: Return is not price; frequencies cannot be averaged directly, simple and log returns diverge in large moves, and bad adjustment or cash-flow handling creates fake returns.

price adjustment

Definition: Price adjustment restates historical prices for splits, dividends and rights issues so return series are continuous and comparable.

Mechanism: Forward adjustment rebases history to the current basis, backward keeps the historical basis; dividend adjustment adds payouts or adjusts prices.

Example: For example, after a 2-for-1 split the price halves, and adjusted history halves too, avoiding a fake fall.

Boundary: Adjusted prices are not actual trades; forward and backward values differ, dividend tax and rights rules affect adjustment, and inconsistent methods break backtests and comparisons.

total return

Definition: Total return includes price change plus reinvested dividends, interest and coupons, reflecting the actual holding return.

Mechanism: Total-return indexes assume reinvestment; price return ignores income, net return deducts taxes, and currency and fees matter.

Example: For example, a 2 percent dividend plus 5 percent price gain gives about 7 percent total return, slightly more with reinvestment.

Boundary: Total return is not price return; reinvestment assumptions, taxes and FX change it, dividend timing and withholding matter, and income assets must be compared on total return.

benchmarks in data

Definition: A benchmark is the reference for a strategy, such as a market index, peer group, liability or cash, matching scope and risk.

Mechanism: Benchmarks affect excess return, attribution and incentives; currency, fees and rebalancing must align.

Example: For example, a China equity strategy uses the CSI 300 total-return index, not the price index.

Boundary: A benchmark is not better when lower; mismatch creates fake alpha, constituent and weight changes affect comparison, and survivorship and fees are often ignored.

futures rolls

Definition: A roll closes an expiring futures contract and moves to a later one, and continuous series must handle the roll jump.

Mechanism: Roll return depends on the curve; continuous contracts use splicing, ratio or Panama methods, producing different histories.

Example: For example, crude futures roll monthly and a contango curve costs long positions at each roll.

Boundary: Rolling is not costless; curve, liquidity and delivery rules create roll P&L, continuous methods can exaggerate or hide returns, and backtests must state roll rules.

trading calendars

Definition: A trading calendar defines market open, close, half days and holidays, affecting alignment, returns and backtests.

Mechanism: Different calendars, time zones and settlement cycles create gaps; cross-market strategies need timestamp alignment to avoid misalignment and fake returns.

Example: For example, China and US holidays differ, so a cross-market portfolio may have prices for only one side on a date.

Boundary: The calendar is not background; missing days, half days and time-zone errors create fake volatility and look-ahead, vendor calendars can be stale, and explicit handling is required.

event and portfolio data

Definition: Event data record corporate actions, earnings, mergers, dividends, splits and delistings, and portfolio data record holdings, weights and trades.

Mechanism: Event data support adjustment, factors and backtests; portfolio data support attribution, risk and capacity, and timing and versions determine correctness.

Example: For example, a merger completion changes shares and index membership, and delisting creates survivorship bias.

Boundary: An event is not effective on the announcement date; effective, ex-date and pay date differ, portfolio data can lag or overlap, and wrong handling causes look-ahead and fake returns.

Exercises

  1. Why must the estimation window exclude the event window?
  2. Does a significant CAR prove that the event caused the return change?
  3. Why does event-day clustering overstate the t value?
Suggested answers
  1. The event would contaminate the α and β estimates, absorbing part of the abnormal return into normal returns.
  2. No. Contemporaneous news, selection or model misspecification may explain it; control windows, alternative benchmarks and robust standard errors are needed.
  3. Firms trading on the same day have correlated returns, so the cross-sectional independence assumption fails, standard errors are understated and t values are too large.
