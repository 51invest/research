Probability Distributions, Expectation and Variance

A probability distribution assigns a probability to each possible outcome; expectation is the long-run average, while variance and standard deviation measure dispersion. They describe an assumed distribution, not the next outcome.

Research question

A hypothetical monthly return has three outcomes: -2%, 0% and +4%, with probabilities 0.25, 0.45 and 0.30. What is the average outcome and how wide is the dispersion? Does an expected value of 0.70% mean next month will return 0.70%? This article separates the distribution, expectation and variance, and states what they can and cannot answer. All numbers are teaching hypotheticals, not real fund data.

Data

Outcome x (%) Probability P(x) Note
-2 0.25 Loss
0 0.45 Flat
+4 0.30 Gain
Total 1.00 Must sum to 1

Outcomes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive; probabilities sum to 1.00. They are assumed, not historical frequencies.

Method and assumptions

A discrete random variable X lists each value and its probability; probabilities lie between 0 and 1 and sum to 1 (OpenStax 4.1). The expected value is the probability-weighted average:

μ = Σ x · P(x)

Variance is the probability-weighted average of squared deviations:

σ² = Σ (x - μ)² · P(x)

The standard deviation is its square root, in percentage points (OpenStax 4.2; NIST defines variance as average squared distance from the mean).

Key assumptions: outcomes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive; probabilities are known and stable over the period; observations are independent and identically distributed; the horizon is one period. Expectation is a long-run average, not the most likely outcome, and need not occur.

Results

μ = 0.25×(-2) + 0.45×0 + 0.30×4 = 0.70 (%).

σ² = 0.25×(-2.70)² + 0.45×(-0.70)² + 0.30×(3.30)² = 1.8225 + 0.2205 + 3.2670 = 5.31 (%²).

σ = √5.31 ≈ 2.30 (percentage points).

The expected 0.70% is not one of the three possible outcomes; 0% is most likely (45%) and the loss probability is 25%. Variance is in squared percentage points and cannot be compared directly with a return, so the 2.30-percentage-point standard deviation is reported as well. Reporting only the expectation hides the 25% loss probability and the 2.30-point dispersion.

Robustness

With probabilities 0.20, 0.50 and 0.30, expectation rises to 0.80% and variance falls to 4.96 %²; values are assumption-sensitive. Adding a -10% tail outcome and scaling the others raises variance sharply; NIST notes that heavy tails distort the standard deviation. With 36 independent observations, the standard error would be about 2.30/√36 ≈ 0.38 points — sampling uncertainty, not distribution dispersion; sampling design and interval estimation follow the main article Sampling and Confidence Intervals (RES-011). If months are correlated, 12-month variance is not 12 times the monthly variance; joint movement of two series is covered in the main article Covariance and Correlation (RES-010).

Limitations

Probabilities are unknown and change with institutions, market conditions and investor behaviour; historical frequencies are not future probabilities. Returns are often skewed and heavy-tailed; variance penalises upside and downside equally and ignores the order of losses. Expectation and variance omit tail losses, path dependence, liquidity and costs. Sample estimates face survivorship bias and multiple testing. The numbers are hypothetical and are not an assessment of any fund.

Reproduction

  1. List mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes; check probabilities sum to 1.
  2. Compute μ.
  3. Weight squared deviations by probability and sum for variance.
  4. Take the square root and state the units (notation follows the main article Data Units and Statistical Definitions, RES-001).
  5. Report probability source, sample size, period and independence.
x = [-2, 0, 4]
p = [0.25, 0.45, 0.30]
mu = sum(xi * pi for xi, pi in zip(x, p))
var = sum(pi * (xi - mu) ** 2 for xi, pi in zip(x, p))
sd = var ** 0.5

Strength of conclusions

Under the assumed distribution, expectation is 0.70% and standard deviation 2.30 percentage points: a descriptive calculation, not an estimate, forecast or causal claim. Sample-based probabilities also need a sampling design and a confidence interval; judgement-based ones hold only under that judgement.

Knowledge-point review

random variables

Definition: A random variable maps outcomes of a random experiment to numbers, either discrete or continuous.

Mechanism: Discrete variables take countable values, continuous ones take interval values with a density; distributions, expectation and variance describe behaviour.

Example: For example, a die roll is discrete and a daily stock return can be modelled as continuous.

Boundary: A random variable is not an arbitrary number; the probability model and sample space define meaning, continuous point probabilities are zero, and discretisation and truncation change distributions.

probability distributions

Definition: A distribution describes probabilities or densities of a random variable, including normal, binomial, Poisson, uniform and heavy-tailed forms.

Mechanism: Mean, variance, skew and tail shape characterise it; normal approximates symmetric data, binomial counts successes, and heavy tails suit extremes.

Example: For example, repeated coin flips are binomial, while financial returns often have fat tails and skew that normal underestimates.

Boundary: Distribution is not a label; wrong assumptions understate risk, mixtures, truncation and time variation defeat one distribution, and fit does not prove the data-generating process.

expectation

Definition: Expectation is the probability-weighted average of a random variable, the long-run average over repeated trials.

Mechanism: Discrete expectation sums values times probabilities, continuous uses an integral; expectation is linear, but the expectation of a product equals the product only under independence.

Example: For example, a fair die has expectation 3.5, and portfolio expected return is weights times asset expectations.

Boundary: Expectation is not the most likely outcome; under heavy tails it can be dominated by extremes or undefined, sample means converge slowly, and expectation is not a realisable return.

variance

Definition: Variance is the average squared deviation from expectation, and standard deviation is its square root, measuring dispersion.

Mechanism: Higher variance means more volatility, standard deviation is interpretable in data units, and covariance and correlation extend variance to multiple variables.

Example: For example, two funds with equal average returns, the lower-variance one is less volatile.

Boundary: Variance is not complete risk; it treats upside and downside alike, ignores tails and skew, can understate extreme losses, and depends on sample and frequency.

covariance

Definition: Covariance measures how two random variables deviate from their expectations together, and correlation is standardised covariance.

Mechanism: Positive covariance means co-movement, negative means opposite; correlation lies between −1 and 1 and is comparable across variables.

Example: For example, stocks and bonds are often negatively correlated, so diversification cuts volatility, while same-sector stocks covary highly.

Boundary: Covariance is not causation; correlation can come from common factors, spurious relations or nonlinearity, rises in crises, and estimates depend on samples and outliers.

sampling

Definition: Sampling selects a subset of a population, using simple random, stratified, cluster or systematic methods.

Mechanism: Method determines representativeness and error; probability sampling allows standard errors, while non-probability sampling is prone to selection bias.

Example: For example, stratified sampling draws firms by sector share, and simple random sampling gives each unit equal chance.

Boundary: Sampling is not arbitrary data collection; poor coverage, survivorship and self-selection bias the sample, large size does not remove bias, and convenience samples cannot infer the population.

estimation and uncertainty

Definition: Estimation infers population parameters from samples: a point estimate gives one value, while intervals and standard errors express uncertainty.

Mechanism: Common estimates include means, proportions, regression coefficients and variances; unbiasedness, consistency and efficiency evaluate estimators, and standard errors reflect sampling variation.

Example: For example, a sample mean estimates the population mean and standard error falls with larger samples.

Boundary: An estimate is not the truth; bias, model error and sampling variation cause deviation, and standard errors cover sampling uncertainty, not bias or misspecification.

Data and institutional check: probability distribution

Before using "probability distribution" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns expected value.

Third, the institutional boundary. variance is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: probability distribution

Learning "probability distribution" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "expected value": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "variance" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of probability distribution still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind expected value reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "probability distribution" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "expected value" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. Change the probabilities to 0.20, 0.50 and 0.40. What is wrong?
  2. If x = 0 with probability 0.6 and x = 10 with probability 0.4, find the expectation, variance and standard deviation.
  3. Two distributions both have an expectation of 5%, with standard deviations of 1% and 8%. What does that mean for an investor?
Suggested answers
  1. They sum to 1.10, not 1; the probabilities must be re-allocated before this set can be used.
  2. μ = 0.6×0 + 0.4×10 = 4; σ² = 0.6×(0-4)² + 0.4×(10-4)² = 9.6 + 14.4 = 24; σ = √24 ≈ 4.90.
  3. With equal expectations, the second is more volatile and carries a higher risk of losses and large drawdowns; expectation alone does not describe risk, so combine it with the standard deviation, tail losses and personal constraints.

  3. Find one rule change in "variance" and explain how it changes sample coverage.
  4. Run a counterfactual with this article's method: change one variable and hold the rest fixed.
  5. If you switched to an independent source, which order of magnitude would move most, and why?
