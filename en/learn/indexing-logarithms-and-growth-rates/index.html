Indexing, Logarithms and Growth Rates

An index turns a level into a ratio to a base period, and a logarithm turns that ratio into additive changes; growth rates, compound annual growth and doubling times are comparable only when definitions, base periods and price bases match.

Research question

The same numbers can be read as an index with a base year of 100, as period growth rates, or as the slope of a logged series. If year 0 has index 100.00 and year 4 has 146.41, the four-year increase is 46.41%; compounding gives 10.00% a year, while the annualized log difference is 9.53%. The question is what each answers, when they can be substituted, and when they mislead. Scope: the arithmetic of indexing and growth rates; all numbers are hypothetical.

Data

Suppose an annual index equals 100.00 in base year 0 and grows 10.00% each year.

Quarterly real GDP growth is annualized by multiplying by four, and only real GDP is used for comparisons across periods — the annualization convention taken from OpenStax [2].

Method and assumptions

Indexing sets I_t = 100 × V_t ÷ V_0, so the base year is always 100; rebasing changes the level, I_t = 100 × V_t ÷ V_b. When two series are compared side by side, first keep the periods both of them have, then rebase both to the same date = 100; the shared starting point is a result of rebasing, not a property of the data. The period growth rate is g_t = V_t ÷ V_{t-1} - 1; the compound annual growth rate is g = (V_T ÷ V_0)^(1/T) - 1, which assumes the same rate compounds every year. The log difference is ln(V_T) - ln(V_0) = ln(V_T ÷ V_0), which equals the sum of period ln(1+g_t) terms; for small annual growth ln(1+g) ≈ g, so annualized log growth is close to the geometric mean. A log scale plots ln(V_t): constant growth is a straight line with slope ln(1+g); equal vertical distances represent equal proportional, not absolute, changes. Doubling time is ln 2 ÷ ln(1+g), approximated by 70 ÷ the percentage growth rate. The examples assume consistent definitions, frequency and price basis; for nominal values, deflate with a price index first.

A price index follows OpenStax: the cost of the base-year basket is set to 100, the current index equals current cost divided by base-year cost times 100, and inflation is (new − old) ÷ old × 100 [1].

Results

The index rises from 100.00 to 146.41, a total increase of 46.41%, and the CAGR is (1.4641)^(1/4) - 1 = 10.00%. The log difference is ln(1.4641) = 0.3812; annualized, 0.3812 ÷ 4 = 0.0953, or 9.53%, which is 0.47 percentage points below 10.00% because of compounding and the log approximation; the gap widens with growth. Rebasing to year 2 gives 82.64 for year 0 and 121.00 for year 4; every level changes, but the year-4-to-year-0 ratio and the period growth rates do not, because rebasing multiplies all levels by the same constant. At 10.00%, the exact doubling time is ln 2 ÷ ln 1.10 = 7.27 years; the rule of 70 gives 7.0 years, a difference of 0.27 years. On a linear axis the annual increment rises from 10.00 to 13.31 and looks larger each year; on a log axis every year adds 0.0953, so the slope is constant.

Robustness

Rebase, change the endpoints and recompute with quarterly or monthly data; compare log differences with simple growth rates; deflate nominal series before comparing; if the series has a break, definition change or reweighting, split the sample and document it. If a claim holds only for one base period or one segment, report that. Failure conditions include zeros or negative values (the log is undefined), a changed basket, reweighting, and splicing indices with different definitions.

The natural logarithm is defined by an integral, logarithms to any base satisfy log_a z = ln z ÷ ln a, and log differences can be used to express proportional change; the log conventions here follow the NIST/SEMATECH handbook [3].

Limitations

Index levels depend on the base period and basket, so levels from different bases are not comparable; a fixed basket has substitution bias, and quality change and coverage affect the reading. CAGR links only the endpoints and hides the path; the same endpoints can come from very different paths. Log differences are symmetric: a 100% rise is +0.693 log points and a 50% fall is -0.693 log points, equal in size but opposite in sign, so readers can mistake log changes for percentages. A log axis cannot show zero or negative values and compresses large values. The numbers are hypothetical and describe no country, market or institution.

Reproduction

Record the series code, base period, units, frequency, price basis, deflator, formula, software version and rounding; keep the raw values and script; check 1.10^4 = 1.4641 and ln(1.4641) = 0.3812; label teaching assumptions separately from observations.

Strength of conclusions

These are index arithmetic and descriptive conversions: the index rose 46.41%, the CAGR was 10.00% and annualized log growth was 9.53%. It is not fair to say growth was steady, that the rate can be extrapolated forward, or that any factor caused it. CAGR is the constant rate connecting two points, not proof about the path; a log axis changes the presentation, not the data.

Exercises

  1. An index rises from 100.00 to 146.41 over four years. What are the total increase and the CAGR?
  2. What is ln(146.41 ÷ 100), and what is the annualized log growth?
  3. At 10.00% growth, what are the exact doubling time and the rule-of-70 estimate?
  4. With year 4 as the base (=100), what is the year-0 index?
Suggested answers
  1. Total increase 146.41 ÷ 100 - 1 = 46.41%; CAGR (1.4641)^(1/4) - 1 = 10.00%.
  2. ln(1.4641) = 0.3812; annualized 0.3812 ÷ 4 = 0.0953 = 9.53%.
  3. Exact ln 2 ÷ ln 1.10 = 7.27 years; rule of 70 70 ÷ 10 = 7.0 years.
  4. 100 ÷ 1.4641 = 68.30.
