Survivorship Bias and Trading Costs

In a simulated 500-fund, 120-month universe, keeping only surviving funds lifts annualized return from 5.7% to 6.6%; a high-turnover strategy earns 9.4% gross but 7.96% after 30 bp one-way costs, only 0.22 percentage points above the low-turnover version.

Research question

Using 500 simulated funds over 120 months and, separately, two turnover versions of a stock signal, this article asks two questions: how much does keeping only funds still present at the end raise the average annualized return? After deducting a one-way trading cost of 30 basis points, does the high-turnover version still beat the low-turnover version? The data are generated with seed 20261035 and are a teaching simulation, not real funds or stocks.

Method and assumptions

Funds are generated monthly, with exit probability negatively related to recent returns: poor performers are more likely to close or merge. The generative process is a teaching model this article defines, produced by one run of the in-repo script scripts/simulate-survivorship.mjs (mulberry32 pseudo-random numbers with a Box-Muller normal, seed 20261035): each fund earns μ = 0.5% plus a σ = 4% normal shock every month; once a fund is 24 months old it exits each month with probability clamp(0.006 − 0.00025 × trailing-12-month annualised return (%), 0.0002, 0.02); the performance window is the last 60 months, annualised as the arithmetic mean of monthly returns times 12, with no geometric compounding. Survivorship bias means counting only units still in the sample at the end and ignoring those that exited; it is a form of sampling bias, in which some members of the population are less likely to be included than others (OpenStax, 1.2). Elton et al. (1996) estimate this bias using fund merger terms, and Barber and Odean (2000) document the net-return penalty of frequent trading; the example simulates both mechanisms. A separate simulation uses two turnover versions of a stock signal. Trading costs are annual one-way turnover times one-way cost; the one-way cost is fixed at 30 basis points and excludes market impact, borrowing fees, taxes and cash drag. The component-by-component split of those costs is in the main article Trade Execution and Slippage (INV-022). Net return is gross return minus cost taken arithmetically, without geometric compounding.

Results

Survivor-only funds earn 6.6% annualized versus 5.7% for the full sample, a gap of 0.9 percentage points. High turnover earns 9.4% gross, pays 1.44% in costs and nets 7.96%; low turnover earns 8.1% gross, pays 0.36% and nets 7.74%. The gross gap is 1.3 percentage points, but the net gap is only 0.22 percentage points.

Robustness

  • At a 50 bp one-way cost, high turnover pays 2.40% and nets 7.00%, while low turnover pays 0.60% and nets 7.50%; high turnover now lags by 0.50 percentage points.
  • At 20 bp, high turnover nets 8.44% and low turnover nets 7.86%, so high turnover leads by 0.58 percentage points.
  • Setting net returns equal, 9.4 - 4.8c = 8.1 - 1.2c gives c ≈ 0.361%, a break-even one-way cost of about 36 bp.
  • A different survival definition changes the gap, but its direction stays the same as long as exits are related to returns (rerun with node scripts/simulate-survivorship.mjs <seed> <funds> <months>; --check compares the script against the printed figures).

Limitations

The simulated funds and stocks are not a real market; costs and turnover are fixed, and market impact, liquidity, borrowing fees, taxes and capacity are omitted. Simulated exits are driven only by recent performance, while real liquidations and mergers can be unrelated to performance, and real data also show backfilling and category changes. Returns and costs are point estimates without confidence intervals. The evidence supports "keep exiting units and deduct costs in a backtest", not any forecast of a real strategy's return.

Reproduction

  1. Run node scripts/simulate-survivorship.mjs: it generates 120 monthly returns and exit states for 500 funds with seed 20261035, the exit probability tied to the trailing twelve-month return as in the model above.
  2. Compute average annualized returns for survivors and for the full sample.
  3. Generate the two turnover versions of the same signal and compute gross returns.
  4. Deduct annual one-way turnover times one-way cost to obtain net returns.
  5. Report the survival rate, cost assumptions and all results.
seed = 20261035

net = gross - turnover_fraction * one_way_cost

Strength of conclusions

Both gaps are simulated; they show the direction and magnitude of two biases, not real performance. The evidence supports keeping exiting units, reporting turnover and deducting costs in a backtest; it does not support treating gross returns as achievable.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Survivorship Bias and Trading Costs"

The subject of this article is "Survivorship Bias and Trading Costs". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "survivorship bias". Column two states the transmission channel of "trading costs". Column three states the institutional boundary of "turnover". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "trading costs" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. turnover is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Review sentence

Write one sentence only: "Under the ____ convention, a change in ____ affects ____ through ____; if ____ appears, the claim fails." The four blanks are the unit, the variable, the channel and the falsifier. If the sentence cannot be written, at least one of the four columns is still empty; if it can be written but cannot be observed, the falsifier is wrong. On every data update, reread this sentence before reading the charts.

Boundary case: Survivorship bias of 0.9 points, trading costs of 1.44 points

Surviving funds show 6.6% annual return versus 5.7% for the full sample, a 0.9-point gap. High-turnover gross return is 9.4%, cost is 1.44%, and net is 7.96%; low-turnover gross is 8.1%, cost 0.36%, and net 7.74%. The gross gap is 1.3 points, but the net gap is only 0.22 points. If one-way cost rises from 30 to 60 basis points, high-turnover cost is 2.88%, net 6.52%, below the low-turnover 7.74%.

The conclusion reverses when high turnover truly produces higher gross alpha: if the gross gap widens to 2.5 points, the high-turnover strategy can still win after costs; if costs are underestimated, net return is overstated. The boundary is that survivorship bias changes the sample while trading costs change net return; both make average fund returns look better than investable outcomes.

These numbers come from the simulation in this article, not fund data.

Reading and review

Survivorship bias and trading cost inflate strategies together. Counting only surviving funds removes failures and lifts average returns; backtests that ignore turnover, spreads and impact lift net returns again. Correct with a complete sample including delistings and closures, estimate impact from size and volatility, and multiply turnover by cost. If capacity is small or trading frequent, cost can consume the entire excess return. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.
