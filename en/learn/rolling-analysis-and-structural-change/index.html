Rolling Analysis and Structural Change

With 0.54 estimated in the first half and 0.20 in the second, a full-sample slope of 0.34 hides the break; rolling paths, break tests and out-of-sample forecasts must be reported together, with the window and break date explained - and a test that fails to reject may simply lack power.

Research question

In a hypothetical relationship the slope is 0.60 for the first 60 months and 0.10 for the next 60. What do a full-sample regression, a rolling window and a break test each show? This article uses 120 simulated months to explain rolling analysis and structural change. The data are generated with seed 20261031 and are not real data.

In N(mu,sigma) the second parameter is the standard deviation, not the variance (this model uses 1, 0.5 and 1 in turn). x and y are monthly teaching series in arbitrary simulated units (dimensionless); a slope reads as the change in y per one-unit change in x, and the rolling statistics are computed on 24-month windows within the 120-month sample.

Method and assumptions

Rolling analysis re-estimates parameters at each point using the most recent W observations, producing a path of coefficients or correlations; shorter windows track change but are noisier. Structural change means the parameters shift at some date. With a known break, the residual sums of squares of the pooled sample and the two subsamples can be compared in an F statistic; the unrestricted model has two intercepts and two slopes, so the numerator degrees of freedom are 2 and the denominator degrees of freedom are 120 − 4 = 116. With an unknown break, Andrews (1993) computes the statistic for every candidate date and takes the supremum, whose distribution is nonstandard. R² is not model validation: residual plots reveal drift and non-constant variance (NIST 4.4.4).

Results

The full-sample slope is 0.34, apparently a moderate positive relationship. Split by regime, the first 60 months give 0.54 and the last 60 give 0.20: the second regime is clearly weaker yet still above its designed 0.10. The known-break test gives F(2,116) = 2.8 with p = 0.065, so constant parameters are not rejected at the 5% level but are at 10%. The 24-month rolling correlation ranges from -0.19 to 0.71 and the rolling slope from -0.15 to 0.83; reporting only the 0.71 window would overstate both the strength and the stability of the relationship. Using the first-60-month model to predict the last 60 months gives an out-of-sample R² of -0.13 against the training-period mean benchmark, worse than using the mean. Every figure above comes from one run of npm run sim:rolling-structure with seed 20261031, and npm run check:sim compares the body against the script output. Re-running over the 400 seeds starting at 20261031 gives a full-sample slope averaging 0.35 (95% range 0.18-0.51), an F statistic averaging 6.71 (range 0.88-15.54) and an out-of-sample R² averaging -0.38 (range -1.19 to 0.11). This draw sits at the low end, so with 60 plus 60 months and noise comparable to the break, the test has power but an unstable one: failing to reject is not evidence that nothing changed.

Robustness

  • Windows of 18 or 36 months make the path noisier or smoother, but the coefficient still falls after the break.
  • Robust standard errors or rank correlations leave the subsample difference in place; the exact values change.
  • Use a supremum test when the break date is unknown and an F test when it is known; if the date was chosen by data mining, the critical values need adjustment.
  • Check whether a subsample is driven by a few observations, and report every window length and break-selection rule.

Limitations

The simulated break is exogenous and abrupt; real structural change may be gradual, repeated, or coincide with a change in volatility. Rolling windows have endpoint effects, and both window length and break date affect the conclusion. A detected break does not explain its cause and should not be treated as a policy or institutional change on its own. Extrapolating to a later period goes beyond the observed range (OpenStax 12.5).

Reproduction

  1. Generate 120 months of x and the two-regime slope with the fixed seed 20261031 (npm run sim:rolling-structure).
  2. Estimate the full-sample and two subsample regressions.
  3. Compute 24-month rolling correlations and slopes.
  4. Run the known-break F test and predict the last 60 months from the first 60.
  5. Report the window length, the break-selection rule and all subsamples.

Strength of conclusions

In the simulation the parameters really do change (designed at 0.60 and 0.10): the full-sample coefficient mixes two regimes, the rolling path is unstable and the out-of-sample forecast fails, while this draw of the F test does not reject constant parameters at 5% - a power limit, not evidence that nothing changed. These are descriptive results. Real breaks need institutional context, and a statistical break alone does not establish causation.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Rolling Analysis and Structural Change"

The subject of this article is "Rolling Analysis and Structural Change". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "rolling window". Column two states the transmission channel of "structural change". Column three states the institutional boundary of "parameter instability". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "structural change" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. parameter instability is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Boundary case: Full-sample 0.34, sub-periods 0.54 and 0.20: where is the break?

Over 120 months, the first 60 have a slope of 0.54 and the second 60 a slope of 0.20, while the full sample is 0.34. A break test gives F(2,116) = 2.8 and p = 0.065: it does not reject at 5% but does at 10%. A 24-month rolling slope ranges from -0.15 to 0.83; picking the 0.83 window exaggerates strength and stability. A 12-month window widens the range and produces more false signals; a 36-month window smooths the break and may hide it.

The conclusion reverses when the break date is chosen from the data: selecting the date after seeing the result invalidates the p-value; pre-registering the date or using a sup-F test is more reliable. The boundary is that rolling analysis describes the path, a break test asks whether parameters changed, and out-of-sample tests ask whether forecasts work. They answer different questions.

These numbers come from the simulation in this article, not market data.

Reading and review

Rolling windows test whether a relationship is stable over time, not whether a model can forecast forever. Too short a window lets noise dominate parameters; too long a window averages away structural change. Read the coefficient path, out-of-sample error and break tests: a persistent shift after a policy change or crisis means the old model failed; a brief jump may remain normal. Report window length, re-estimation frequency and trading cost to judge implementability. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Exercises

  1. Why is the full-sample slope of 0.34 below the first-regime 0.54?
  2. What does a wide range of rolling correlations indicate?
  3. How do known-break and unknown-break tests differ?
Suggested answers
  1. The full sample pools both regimes, so the near-zero second slope pulls the average down; the weights also depend on the variance of x in each regime.
  2. The relationship changes over time; a single full-sample correlation hides the instability, and the window choice affects what is seen.
  3. A known break allows a direct comparison of subsample residuals; an unknown break takes the maximum over candidate dates, with larger critical values and a nonstandard distribution.
