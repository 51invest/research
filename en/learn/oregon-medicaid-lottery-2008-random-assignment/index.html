The 2008 Oregon Medicaid Lottery: Random Assignment and Policy Evaluation

In 2008 Oregon used a lottery to select about 30,000 people from a waiting list of nearly 90,000, and about 10,000 enrolled. An administrative necessity became a randomised experiment, letting researchers estimate the causal effect of Medicaid on visits, prescriptions and debt.

Background and timeline

In early 2008, Oregon’s OHP Standard had about 10,000 open slots; the state expected demand to far exceed supply and asked the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services for permission to allocate slots by random lottery from a waiting list. From 28 January to 29 February 2008, residents could sign up by phone, fax, in person, mail or online; 89,824 people entered the list[4]. The state ran eight lottery drawings from March to September, each selecting roughly equal numbers; about 30,000 people, representing about 29,000 households, were selected.

Selection gave the opportunity to apply, not automatic coverage; people had to submit an application within 45 days and prove income, asset and insurance eligibility. About 30% of those selected enrolled, roughly 10,000 people[4]. Researchers compared selected and unselected people, using the lottery’s randomness to estimate causal effects.

First-year results: the probability of outpatient use rose about 35%, prescription use about 15%, hospital admission about 30%, and the probability of borrowing money or skipping payment because of medical costs fell about 40%[4].

Information available and actors

The main actors were the Oregon state government, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, people on the waiting list, the managed-care organisations running the plan, and the research team. The state’s decision was how to allocate slots under a budget cap; a lottery is not clinically optimal, but it provides verifiable randomness. Residents decided in two steps: first to join the waiting list, then to apply after selection.

Sign-up required little verification, lowering the entry barrier; application required income and asset evidence, creating a “selection–application–enrolment” funnel. A 30% enrolment rate shows that lottery assignment is not actual treatment. Researchers decided between intention-to-treat and actual enrolment.

Intention-to-treat compares “selected” with “not selected” and preserves randomness; actual enrolment compares “enrolled” with “not enrolled”, but enrollees chose to apply and may differ in other ways. The two estimates answer different questions and must not be mixed.

Outcome and the number chain

Key numbers: 89,824 on the waiting list; eight drawings; about 30,000 selected; about 10,000 enrolled, a take-up rate of about 30%; outpatient +35%, prescriptions +15%, admissions +30%, borrowing/skipping −40%[4]. Chain them: 10,000 slots → 89,824 demand → about 30,000 selected → about 10,000 applied and qualified → compare selected with unselected. Each step changes the sample: sign-ups are not all eligible, selected are not all enrolled, and enrollees are not all low-income people.

The 30% is “enrolment among the selected”, not “enrolment among the waiting list”. Multiplying 30% by 30,000 gives about 9,000–10,000, matching the public numbers; multiplying 30% by 89,824 gives about 27,000, which wrongly inflates enrolment.

Mechanism

The first layer is random assignment. The lottery makes “selected” and “not selected” roughly balanced in observed and unobserved characteristics, separating the effect of insurance from other factors. The second is intention-to-treat.

Selection only gives an opportunity to apply, with about 30% take-up; the estimate is the effect of “being offered the chance”, not “being insured”. The third is external validity. Oregon’s waiting list, income threshold, benefit design and medical market are specific; extrapolating to the nation, other populations or other plans requires extra assumptions[4].

The three layers create a “natural experiment”: an administrative necessity produced randomness; randomness allows causal estimation; but randomness does not solve extrapolation. A policymaker who treats “the effect on Oregon enrollees” as “the effect of national expansion” ignores take-up, provider supply and population differences.

Counterfactuals and alternative explanations

First, if Oregon had enough budget to cover all 89,824, no lottery would be needed and there would be no randomised experiment; the policy effect might be easier to see, but causal identification harder. Second, if take-up were 100% rather than 30%, the intention-to-treat estimate would be closer to the actual enrolment effect, but the application funnel’s selection issue would disappear and external validity would change. Third, if the plan covered different services (for example, no prescription drugs or mental health), results would differ; OHP Standard included prescriptions and low cost-sharing and cannot represent all Medicaid plans.

Fourth, with only a six-month follow-up, hospital and debt effects might not appear; first-year results cannot be extrapolated to long-term health.

Limits to generalization

The case can show that a random lottery can produce usable causal identification inside an administrative quota; intention-to-treat and actual enrolment answer different questions; take-up, the application funnel and provider supply change the size of policy effects. It cannot show that insurance has the same effect for everyone; that Oregon’s results replicate nationally; that lotteries are the morally best way to allocate coverage; or that first-year results are long-term health conclusions. The value is in the method, not a slogan for or against Medicaid.

Reproduce by listing waiting-list size, number of drawings, selected and enrolled counts; then eligibility, application steps and take-up; then outcomes (outpatient, prescriptions, admissions, debt) and follow-up; finally four external-validity conditions. Decision review

From a state government’s view, the lottery was administrative allocation under a budget constraint, not a moral optimum. It avoided queues, connections and political allocation but handed “who gets coverage” to randomness. A government can expand slots, raise take-up or cut cost, but cannot maximise all three; the lottery answers “how to allocate fixed slots”, not “how many slots there should be”.

From a researcher’s view, the lottery provides randomness, but the funnel between “selected” and “enrolled” dilutes the effect. Intention-to-treat estimates the effect of “offering the chance” and suits policy evaluation; actual enrolment needs instruments or other methods and suits estimating insurance itself. Researchers must state which question they answer and never mix the two.

From an extrapolation view, Oregon’s waiting list, income threshold, benefit design and provider supply are specific. National expansion could change supply, queues and prices, so effects may differ. Treating Oregon’s results as a national forecast requires extra assumptions and validation, not simple number copying.

Review record

Fact layer: checked waiting list, number of drawings, selected, take-up and first-year outcomes against the paper. Number layer: separated sign-up, selection, application and enrolment, never multiplying 30% by the wrong base. Mechanism layer: confirmed random assignment, intention-to-treat and extrapolation.

Counterfactual layer: changed slots, take-up, benefit design and follow-up one at a time. Boundary layer: made clear that Oregon does not directly represent the nation.
