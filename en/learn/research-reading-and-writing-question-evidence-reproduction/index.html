Research Reading and Writing: Question, Evidence and Reproduction

Good research is not “opinion plus data” but a question that can be answered, evidence that can be checked and a conclusion with boundaries. When reading, start with the question and identification, then the conclusion; when writing, make data, method, robustness and limits reproducible.

Research question

Is a study worth reading? First check whether the question is answerable, the evidence supports it, identification is credible and the conclusion stays within bounds. This article offers a reading and writing checklist; the point is not “is the conclusion right” but “can readers judge under what conditions it holds”[1].

Data

When reading, record data source, sample, time, variable definitions and missing-data treatment; when writing, provide the same. Data-quality frameworks (DQAF) stress completeness, accuracy, timeliness and accessibility; these dimensions also apply to research data[2].

Method

Split a study into four steps: question, evidence, identification, conclusion. Evidence levels can be ranked by design: systematic reviews and randomised trials are stronger, cohorts and cases weaker; but level is not strength, because sample, execution and bias matter. GRADE uses certainty ratings to remind us that high-level evidence can be downgraded for bias[3].

Results

Suppose a study uses a case to argue that “a policy promoted growth” without a control, time series or exclusion of external shocks. The conclusion can only be “improvement was observed in this case”, not “the policy works generally”. If another study uses a randomised trial replicated across sites, the conclusion is stronger but external validity still needs checking.

Robustness

When reading, check whether conclusions hold across samples, controls, time windows and identification strategies; when writing, report these tests. Robustness is not an appendix but part of the conclusion. If the author reports only one significant result, readers should ask about other specifications.

Limitations

First, an unanswerable question; second, unverifiable data; third, invalid identification; fourth, conclusions beyond the sample; fifth, confusion between evidence level and strength; sixth, missing reproduction information. Under these conditions, a study is a clue, not a verdict.

Reproduction

When writing, provide data, code, variable dictionary, model specification and robustness tables; when reading, check the same list. The goal is not to repeat the author’s conclusion but to let third parties judge the conditions under which it holds.

Reading and review

Reading research starts by reconstructing the problem, identification, evidence and boundary rather than treating the abstract as the conclusion. Is the question answerable, does the design rule out the main alternatives, does the evidence support magnitude and not only sign, and does the boundary state sample, period and external validity? Writing reverses the order: state the question and testable prediction, then data and identification, then conclusion and failure conditions. Reproduction is not an appendix but part of the argument: data version, code, parameters and seeds belong in the text; without them a reader cannot tell a result from a choice. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Reading checklist

Make failure conditions operational. Do not write “the sample may be small”; write “with fewer than 30 observations the confidence interval exceeds 20% and the result is not used for decisions”. Operational boundaries tell readers when to stop and tell replicators which parameters to record.

Further check

Before applying “Research Reading and Writing: Question, Evidence and Reproduction”, test the weakest link between “Question and identification” and “Evidence level and strength”. Write down the observable proxy, the direction of the causal link and the condition under which the link reverses. Then ask whether the data are measured at the same frequency and definition as the decision, and whether a cost or lag sits between the signal and the outcome. If the chain cannot be stated without hand-waving, the result is a description, not a usable claim. Run one counterexample and one stress case before extending the conclusion to another market, period or population.

Working paper: a four-column check for "Research Reading and Writing: Question, Evidence and Reproduction"

The subject of this article is "Research reading and writing: issues, evidence and replication". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Question and identification". Column two states the transmission channel of "Evidence level and strength". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Writing structure and reproduction". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Evidence level and strength" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Writing structure and reproduction is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Review sentence

Write one sentence only: "Under the ____ convention, a change in ____ affects ____ through ____; if ____ appears, the claim fails." The four blanks are the unit, the variable, the channel and the falsifier. If the sentence cannot be written, at least one of the four columns is still empty; if it can be written but cannot be observed, the falsifier is wrong. On every data update, reread this sentence before reading the charts.

Boundary case: One case shows improvement: can it prove the policy works?

A locality adopts a policy and its indicator rises from 100 to 110, a 10% gain. If the national indicator rises from 100 to 108 over the same period, the 2-point difference may be a common trend. Without a control, a time series or a check for external shocks, the writer can only say “improvement was observed in this case”. If a randomised trial is replicated at two sites with effects of 10% and 6%, averaging 8%, external validity still needs checking.

The conclusion reverses with a control and pre-registration: if the control group changes only 1%, a 9-point effect is more credible; if the sample covers one city, it still cannot be generalised nationwide. The boundary is that question, evidence, identification and replication are four separate things. The strength of a conclusion is set by design, not by wording.

Exercises

  1. Reading question: a study has only a case and no control. How far can the conclusion go?
  2. Evidence question: randomised trial, cohort study and case report — what evidence levels are they, and how do they rank?
  3. Writing question: list three pieces of information needed for reproduction and explain why each is necessary.
  4. Explain the causal chain between “Question and identification” and “Evidence level and strength” with the article's framework, and name one observable indicator.
  5. Change the base-case number to another value, recompute once, and explain the direction and size of the result.
  6. List at least three boundary conditions and say which one is most often ignored.
  7. Construct a counterexample in which the article's conclusion fails; state the conditions it needs.
  8. If you could keep only one check, which would it be, and how does it prevent the worst error?
