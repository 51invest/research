Triangulation across Sources: Satellite, Text and Administrative Data

Every single source has blind spots: satellites miss underground activity, text has selection bias and administrative data reflect reporting incentives. Triangulation is not 'using more sources' but cross-checking independent error structures to bound the true value.

Research problem and question

Triangulation measures one phenomenon with multiple independent sources so that complementary error structures narrow uncertainty. It is not 'using more sources' but requiring uncorrelated errors across sources.

Blind spots: satellites (night lights, crop remote sensing) offer broad, high-frequency coverage but miss underground activity, services and income distribution; text (news, contracts, social media) is rich but selected and platform-biased; administrative data (tax, social security, registries) cover specific populations but reflect reporting incentives and definition changes.

The core: if three sources point to the same interval, confidence rises; if they diverge, explain the divergence rather than averaging it away[1][2].

Data and worked example

Suppose we estimate regional economic growth. Night lights suggest 4%, text sentiment 6% and tax registrations 3%. The gap is large; averaging to 4.3% is wrong.

Check errors: lights may reflect electrification and prices; text may reflect media bias; tax records may miss the informal sector. If lights and tax records cover the same formal sector, their errors are correlated and do not triangulate.

If the three are independent and each gives a 3%-6% range, report a 4%-5% intersection or weighted median with uncertainty. The goal is a credible interval, not a precise point. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Inputs and assumptions

Triangulation needs three input families: satellite imagery (night lights, NDVI, built-up footprints), text corpora (news, contracts, social platforms) and administrative records (tax, social security, business registers). Each input carries a geocode, timestamp, unit, coverage and collection rule; align administrative boundaries, firm identifiers and person identifiers before claiming mutual verification. Three assumptions matter: the sources measure the same economic construct, their errors are independent, and reporting definitions are stable over the comparison window. If any fails, triangulation degenerates into collecting more numbers.

Method and steps

Check 1: independence. Do sources share underlying data, collection processes or stakeholders? More sharing means less validation value.

Check 2: coverage consistency. Do they measure the same population, period and definition? Align definitions first.

Check 3: error direction. Is each source biased up or down? If all bias up, averaging amplifies it; correct separately.

Record data versions, cleaning rules and uncertainty; reproducibility matters more than the number of sources[3][4].

Failure conditions and boundaries

Boundary 1: multiple sources are not independent. If all three cite the same statistic, they are not independent validation; check collection.

Boundary 2: satellites are not universal. Lights and remote sensing measure activity and land change, not income distribution, service quality or institutions; combine with other sources.

Boundary 3: text has narrative bias. News and social media reflect platforms, language and events; popularity is not fact.

Mistake 1: averaging three sources is triangulation. Averaging hides disagreement; check independence and error direction first.

Mistake 2: administrative data are most accurate. They cover specific populations, and under-reporting and incentives create systematic bias; they are not truth.

Interpreting the results: triangulation, satellite data and administrative data

Triangulation is not 'find more data sources' but validating one construct with independent measurements. Satellite imagery, text, administrative records and surveys each carry different errors; if three point to the same conclusion, confidence rises; if they diverge, explain why.

Satellite data offer broad coverage, high frequency and history; weaknesses are resolution, clouds, algorithms and ground truth. Night lights, crop NDVI, building footprints and ship AIS need calibration; lights and economic activity are non-linear, not a direct GDP proxy.

Text data (news, filings, social media) capture expectations and sentiment but are shaped by platform selection, language, censorship and algorithms; word frequency is not an economic variable and needs human labels, topic models and external validation.

Administrative data (tax, social security, customs, registers) cover the population with clear definitions but may lag, change definitions or be access-restricted; differences from survey definitions make them incomparable.

Matching and privacy: micro-matching needs identifiers and is restricted by privacy law; differential privacy, aggregation and synthetic data reduce risk but lose precision.

Triangulation design: define the construct and error structure first, then choose independent sources; sources derived from the same survey are not independent validation.

Measurement: coverage, frequency, error, definition consistency and match rate. Precision of one source is not overall credibility.

Common misuses: night lights as GDP, word frequency as sentiment, administrative data as error-free, and related sources as independent validation.

Finally, triangulation works when errors are uncorrelated; if errors share a source, more data only repeats the same bias.

Reproduction and checklist

A reproduction checklist should record data versions and download dates, image cloud cover and preprocessing, text keywords and deduplication, administrative reporting rules and revisions, geographic and temporal alignment, missing-data handling, interval-combination rules, and sources of uncertainty. Code should regenerate the same interval table from raw files; where judgement is involved, state the rule and the reviewer. Reproduction does not demand an identical point estimate; it lets readers inspect how each source entered the final interval.

Multi-source checklist

When handed a 'multi-source verification' conclusion, ask five questions:

  1. Are the sources truly independent? Shared underlying data, one collection agency or one stakeholder turns 'multiple sources' into copies of the same error.
  2. Do they measure the same construct? Night lights, text sentiment and tax records may proxy activity, confidence and the formal sector; without alignment, intersection is meaningless.
  3. Is the error direction known? Which way and under what conditions each source is biased; if all bias upward, averaging amplifies it.
  4. How is the interval combined? Intersection, weighted median and Bayesian updating carry different assumptions; report uncertainty sources and weights.
  5. Can it be reproduced? Data versions, cleaning rules, geographic alignment and code; an unreproducible multi-source claim is several stories.

Triangulation is independent errors checking each other, not a count of sources.

What would change the conclusion

Triangulation conclusions depend on independence and definitions:

  1. Sources share underlying data. If satellite, text and administrative data come from one collection system or agency, errors are highly correlated and verification adds little.
  2. Constructs do not align. If lights proxy activity, text proxies sentiment and tax records proxy the formal sector, they do not measure the same thing and intersection is meaningless.
  3. Error direction is unknown. If all three sources bias upward, averaging amplifies rather than reduces error.
  4. Interval combination is wrong. If independent intervals are simply averaged without weights and correlation, uncertainty is understated.
  5. It cannot be reproduced. If data versions, cleaning rules and code are closed, a multi-source conclusion is several stories.

Practical implication: multi-source verification should establish independence, then construct alignment, then combine intervals; the number of sources is not information, independent errors are what counts.

Data note: report missingness, coverage and error direction for each source; when missingness is not random, interval combination is biased.

Scope note: report missingness and coverage by group; an overall rate hides bias in small regions and high-income groups.

Final note: report missingness by group; an overall rate hides bias in small regions and high-income groups.

Further reading: satellite, text and administrative-data research

Satellite economics: Henderson and co-authors validated night lights against GDP and urbanisation; Chen and Nordhaus calibrated regional output with lights; crop NDVI, ship AIS and building footprints are used for trade, activity and infrastructure.

Text as data: Gentzkow, Kelly and Taddy survey text in economics; word counts, topic models and embeddings need human validation and cannot treat 'sentiment' directly as a variable.

Administrative data: Chetty and co-authors use tax records to study mobility, neighbourhoods and intergenerational transmission; administrative data offer full samples but raise definition, access and privacy issues.

Triangulation: mixed-methods research stresses independent errors and construct consistency; multi-source estimates should report ranges, disagreement and explanations, not simple averages.

Reading suggestion: read one classic application each of satellite, text and administrative data, then design your own triangulation around error structure, not data quantity.

Chapter takeaways

  • Triangulation requires independent errors; more sources is not automatically better.
  • Satellite data cover widely but are limited by resolution, clouds, algorithms and ground truth.
  • Text data are shaped by platform, language, censorship and algorithms; word counts are not variables.
  • Administrative data cover populations but may lag, change definitions or restrict access.
  • Report ranges, disagreement and explanations rather than simple averages.

The subject of this article is "Triangulation across Sources: Satellite, Text and Administrative Data". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Multi-source measurement". Column two states the transmission channel of "Triangulation". Column three states the institutional boundary of "the institutional boundary". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Triangulation" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.
