How to handle data and judge evidence.

“How strong a conclusion does the data support?”

Data definitions, statistical description, estimation, causal inference, forecasting and reproducible research.

14 modules 35 articles about 3 hr 39 min total

Station brief

Start from a concrete scene, then set out the questions, methods and boundaries of this station.

The same GDP release can be written as “growth beat expectations” or “the recovery is weak”. The difference often lies not in the number but in year-on-year versus quarter-on-quarter, seasonal adjustment, or a definition change. Research methods start with those apparently small questions.

Given data, ask about units, denominator, frequency, adjustment and revisions. Stock or flow, nominal or real, national or regional: each choice changes the conclusion.

Charts turn numbers into position. Axis ranges, dual axes, truncation and log scales can change the relationship a reader sees without changing a single datum. Read the axis first, then the legend, then the notes.

Statistics report intervals and uncertainty. Significance says a sample result is inconsistent with some hypothesis; it does not say the effect is large, important or real. Confidence intervals, sample design and multiple testing decide how far the claim travels.

Causal identification is harder than a regression coefficient. Randomised trials are the benchmark; observational work relies on natural experiments, thresholds, instruments and other designs. The wrong control variable can create bias rather than remove it.

After this station, you should be able to take a research report, name its data definitions, chart traps, identification assumptions and out-of-sample evidence, and say how far the conclusion goes.

After this station, you should be able to answer

  • Why can two growth rates not be compared directly?
  • Why do control variables not automatically prove causation?
  • Why does out-of-sample testing matter more than a backtest return?

Four common misreadings

These claims come up again and again in reader mail, comments and working discussions. Naming them is more useful than repeating definitions.

“Data do not lie.”

Data are shaped by definitions, collection, missingness, revisions and disclosure. A clean-looking number may hide a changed definition, an imputed gap or a restated history.

“Significant means important.”

Significance answers whether the evidence is strong enough to reject a null. It does not say how large the effect is, what it costs or whether it generalises. With a large sample, a tiny difference can be significant.

“More controls make the result more credible.”

Controls belong on a causal diagram where they block confounding paths. Controlling a mediator, an outcome or a collider can introduce new bias. More variables do not mean better identification.

“A backtest proves the strategy.”

A backtest must guard against look-ahead, survivorship, multiple trials and underestimated costs, then be tested out of sample. Historical return is part of the evidence, not a promise about the future.

A backtest that claims 20% a year

Three or four articles from this station, applied to one decision. Every number is a teaching assumption for practising the order of judgement, not an estimate of a real market.

A backtest reports 20% a year from 2010 to 2024, with a 15% maximum drawdown. Start with the sample: how many delisted companies are included? Keeping only the firms that survived today biases returns upward.

Then ask how many parameter sets were tried. If 200 were tested and the best was kept, 20% may be data mining. Look for out-of-sample results, rolling tests, and whether the return survives a small change in parameters.

Count costs. Turnover of 300% at 0.1% one-way costs about 0.6 percentage points a year; add market impact and the strategy’s capacity may be small.

Finally, run the counterfactual: remove the five best months. If the return falls to 5%, the strategy depends on a few events and is not stable compounding. Put these three checks in the report; they are worth more than another equity curve.

Key terms

These words recur throughout the station. Learn the sense used here before moving into the articles.

data unit 数据单位

A number needs a unit; a ratio also needs its denominator, period and price basis.

RES-001 · Data Units and Statistical Definitions
stocks and flows 存量与流量

A stock is a balance at a point in time; a flow is a change over a period. A deficit is a flow; debt is a stock.

RES-002 · Stocks and Flows
seasonal adjustment 季节调整

Removes regular seasonal and calendar effects; adjusted and unadjusted numbers cannot be mixed.

RES-004 · Seasonal Adjustment and Revisions
confidence interval 置信区间

A range for a population parameter built from the sample and its sampling error; 95% means about 95% of repeated intervals cover the true value.

RES-011 · Sampling and Confidence Intervals
p-value p 值

The probability of the observed or a more extreme result if the null is true; it does not give the effect size or the probability that the claim is true.

RES-012 · Hypothesis Testing and Practical Significance
out-of-sample 样本外

Data not used to select the model or fit its parameters; out-of-sample performance is closer to real use.
